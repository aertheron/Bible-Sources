# WT006 — Holiness and sanctification

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** heiligheid, heiliging.

**Original terms:** Hebrew קָדוֹשׁ (qadosh), קָדַשׁ (qadash); Greek ἅγιος (hagios), ἁγιάζω (hagiazō), ἁγιασμός (hagiasmos).

**Default explanation:** Holy is not exhausted by the gloss separate, and sanctification is not always a reference to a later stage in a modern salvation scheme.

**Translation key:** Distinguish holy identity, dedication, consecration, and the activity or result of sanctifying; retain the actual grammatical form.

**Initial orientation:** Leviticus 19 connects God's holiness with Israel's conduct. First Corinthians 1:2 addresses people sanctified in Christ. First Thessalonians 4:3-8 applies sanctification to bodily and sexual conduct.

#### Local reading

Holiness concerns what is holy or consecrated in relation to God; its particular expression depends on the subject. Holy space, holy objects, holy people, and God's holiness are not identical applications. Calling an object holy can identify a consecrated use without attributing a moral character to the object. Calling God holy cannot simply mean that someone else has set God aside for a purpose. The familiar explanation “set apart” is therefore useful but incomplete.

Leviticus 19:1–2 grounds Israel's vocation in Yahweh's holiness. The chapter develops that vocation through family responsibilities, worship, treatment of neighbors, economic practices, and justice. Reading only the opening command could leave holiness as an abstract separation from society, whereas the literary unit gives it concrete social expression. Ritual and moral matters both belong to this textual setting; they should not be collapsed into one undifferentiated rule for every later context.

First Corinthians 1:2 addresses a community described as sanctified in Christ and called holy, even though the letter confronts serious misconduct. First Corinthians 6:9–11 again speaks of washing, sanctifying, and justifying as a changed identity. First Thessalonians 4:3–8 describes sanctification in relation to sexual conduct and treatment of others. These uses support both given consecration and a required way of living; a modern sequence of salvation stages should not be used as the dictionary definition.

#### Translation and default explanation

Translate hagios as holy, sacred, or a contextual designation of God's people. “Saints” can be retained if explained as the community addressed rather than an elite class canonized after death. Hagiazō may mean consecrate, sanctify, or treat as holy. Its use in a prayer that God's name be hallowed is not a request that God become morally better.

The default explanation is that holiness identifies belonging and orientation to God, with the text specifying the required expression. For a person, ethical conduct can be integral to that vocation. For a place or object, dedicated use is foregrounded. Interlinear records must preserve the difference between adjective, verb, and the hagiasmos noun, and should not manufacture a uniform translation from their shared family.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The useful trajectory is holy presence, consecrated community, and life shaped by the Spirit. Hebrews 10:10–14 connects sanctification with Christ's offering and describes its relation to an ongoing sanctifying process. That connection should be explained from the clauses rather than reduced to a slogan about either completed status or gradual growth.

The working synthesis holds identity and vocation together. Grace does not make conduct irrelevant, and conduct does not become the lexical definition of every holy term. Theology review should ask what the passage says has been given, what it commands, and how Christ or the Spirit supplies the connection. Avoid equating holiness with social withdrawal or treating separation as its complete theological content.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Trace God's holy presence and a people's vocation into Spirit-enabled life. Discuss status and growth from passages rather than assuming one excludes the other.

### Full-depth semantic inventory

Hebrew קָדוֹשׁ (*qadosh*), קָדַשׁ (*qadash*), Greek ἅγιος (*hagios*), ἁγιάζω (*hagiazō*) and ἁγιασμός (*hagiasmos*) form overlapping adjective/verb/noun families. The **referent** determines the contextual force: Yahweh is holy; objects, time and places can be designated holy; a community is sanctified or called holy; people can act in holiness. Neither “morally perfect” nor “set apart” fits each of these uses without qualification.

**Contextual participant matrix.** In Lev 19:1–18 Yahweh's holiness grounds instructions about worship, family, economic fairness and protection from exploitation; set-apart identity is inseparable from actual justice. In 1 Cor 1:2 Paul addresses an **already sanctified** congregation while the letter subsequently rebukes its misconduct; status does not prove present moral perfection. In 1 Thess 4:3–8 the divine will for sanctification is applied to concrete sexual conduct and relationships. Hallowing God's name asks people to treat or acknowledge what is already holy as holy: God is not the patient receiving moral improvement.

**Canonical theological distinction.** Consecration by God's initiative, morally shaped life and the community's calling are related, but “positional” and “progressive” are explanatory categories rather than two lexical definitions of *hagiazō*. Heb 10:10–14 holds Christ's decisive offering and continued sanctifying language in one argument and should be read in context rather than split into isolated proof texts. The typical “separation” explanation does not alone account for the weight and otherness of divine holiness. **Coverage limit:** individual priestly purification and cultic consecration categories demand their own local reading; this is a bounded sense map.
