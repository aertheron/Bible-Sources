# WT009 — Nephesh, psychē, soul, life, person

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** nephesh, psychē, ziel, leven, persoon.

**Original terms:** Hebrew נֶפֶשׁ (nephesh); Greek ψυχή (psychē). They are separate lemmas with overlapping contextual uses, not universally equivalent technical terms.

**Default explanation:** Neither always an immortal immaterial component nor always nothing more than a physical body is an adequate automatic lexical rule. Genre and syntax matter.

**Translation key:** Consider life, person, self, or soul from the actual clause. Retain nephesh or psychē with a contextual gloss where soul would import an unexplained theory of human nature.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 2:7 describes the human becoming a living nephesh. Leviticus 17 relates life to blood. Matthew 16 plays on saving and losing one's life. Revelation 6 is a vision of those killed for their testimony.

#### Local reading

Nephesh and psychē can concern life, a person, the self, desire, or what is commonly called soul. They are separate Hebrew and Greek lemmas with overlapping uses. Genesis 2:7 says the human became a living nephesh after the formation and breathing described in the narrative. Living-creature language also applies to animals in Genesis 1:20–24. This supports reading the Genesis phrase as a living being, rather than as a technical statement that an independently immortal component was placed inside a body.

Leviticus 17:10–14 relates the life of flesh to blood and explains a prohibition within a sacrificial setting. Its concern is not a modern anatomical definition of every nephesh occurrence. Matthew 16:24–27 places psychē within the paradox of saving and losing one's life in discipleship. Replacing it mechanically with “immortal soul” would import a conclusion that this wording alone does not establish; replacing it with “physical organism” everywhere would be equally inflexible.

The strongest complicating passages deserve attention. Genesis 35:18 describes Rachel's nephesh going out as she dies. First Kings 17:21–22 describes the child's nephesh returning. Matthew 10:28 distinguishes the body's death from the psychē in a judgment saying. Revelation 6:9–11 depicts the psychai of those killed for their testimony speaking beneath the altar. These are not all the same genre or construction. They must be examined rather than suppressed by a preferred anthropology.

#### Translation and default explanation

“Life,” “person,” or “self” often communicates a local sense well. Retain nephesh or psychē with a short gloss when an English or Dutch soul term would conceal the interpretive issue. “Soul” remains possible where the passage's distinction or traditional rendering needs to remain visible, but a note should say what that word is being used to express.

The default explanation is that neither lemma is an automatic technical term for a naturally immortal, immaterial substance. Neither is an automatic synonym for physical body. A lexicon's range supplies candidates; syntax and setting select the local sense. Assertions about what happens after death require the passages' claims and wider reasoning, not merely a replacement gloss.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A useful trajectory connects created living persons, the vulnerability of life and death, and resurrection hope. The project's emphasis on embodied persons is well served by Genesis and by the New Testament's bodily resurrection language. Its adequacy should be tested against the departure, return, and postmortem-vision passages rather than announced as an exhaustive dictionary definition.

The working conclusion treats embodiment and dependence on God's life-giving action as central. Distinguish that conclusion from narrower lexical observations. Theology review must separate natural immortality, continued personal existence, the intermediate state, and resurrection: denying one proposition does not automatically disprove or establish the others. For a user's passage, retrieve only the sense and comparison that actually clarify its claim.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Separate word meaning from the broader questions of embodiment, death, the intermediate state, and resurrection; test the project's anthropology across the selected texts.
