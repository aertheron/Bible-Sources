# WT024 — Anger, patience, slow to anger

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Name and phrase. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** toorn, geduld, langzaam tot toorn.

**Original terms:** Hebrew אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם (erekh appayim); Greek ὀργή (orgē), μακροθυμία (makrothymia). An idiom and several nouns require separate records.

**Default explanation:** Slow to anger does not mean no judgment, and a literal nose gloss should not replace the idiom's sense in a working translation.

**Translation key:** Translate the Hebrew idiom idiomatically and explain its literal wording in a note. Identify anger, wrath, patience, and forbearance rather than collapsing them.

**Initial orientation:** Exodus 34 includes slow to anger among God's qualities. Proverbs 14:29 contrasts patience and quick temper. Romans 2:4 places patience and kindness beside repentance and judgment.

#### Local reading

Erekh appayim is a Hebrew idiom conventionally rendered “slow to anger.” Its component words involve length and nose/anger vocabulary, but the phrase's communicated sense concerns patience or restraint. A source-order interlinear can show the components while a readable translation gives the idiom. Translating it merely as “long of nostrils” without explanation would leave most readers with an inaccurate impression.

Exodus 34:6–7 places patience alongside compassion, grace, loyal love, faithfulness, forgiveness, and the treatment of wrongdoing. The portrait cannot be reduced either to anger without patience or to patience without judgment. Numbers 14:17–20 reuses these qualities within Moses' appeal after Israel's failure, demonstrating their significance for a concrete covenant crisis rather than only as a list of attributes.

Proverbs 14:29 relates slowness to anger with understanding. Its practical setting concerns human conduct and discernment, not an exhaustive doctrine of divine emotion. Romans 2:4–5 describes God's kindness and patience as leading toward repentance while warning against hardness that stores up wrath. The transition is part of the argument: patience is purposeful and morally consequential, not proof that wrongdoing has become inconsequential.

#### Translation and default explanation

Render the idiom as slow to anger, patient, or a target-language expression with the same force. Dutch *lankmoedig* may require explanation; *langzaam tot toorn* preserves the familiar biblical shape but may be less natural for some readers. Record the source components without treating a literal gloss as the idiom's whole meaning.

The default explanation is that biblical patience describes restraint and endurance in the relevant relationship, allowing time for response. It does not entail absence of anger or judgment. Greek orgē concerns anger or wrath; makrothymia concerns patience or forbearance. A shared context does not make them opposites in every possible sense or justify assigning one only to humans and the other only to God.

#### Biblical and canonical development

Psalm 103:8–14 relates divine patience to compassion and human frailty. Second Peter 3:8–10 places patience within a response to questions about the promised coming and judgment. These provide a focused trajectory concerning delay, opportunity, and fulfillment. The text's announced judgment must remain visible even when an explanation emphasizes the generosity of the delay.

The working synthesis portrays God's response to evil as purposeful rather than impulsive, while allowing the passages' particular claims about anger to stand. A theology review should distinguish the communicated human idiom from conclusions about the mechanics of divine emotional experience. Modern psychological descriptions may illustrate an application but cannot become the translation. For human conduct, patience is not passivity toward harm: the passage's context must identify what restraint, justice, and appropriate action require.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Read patience and moral judgment together rather than using either to cancel the other.
