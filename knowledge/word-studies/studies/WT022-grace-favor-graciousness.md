# WT022 — Grace, favor, graciousness

**Original terms:** Hebrew חֵן (chen), חַנּוּן (channun), חָנַן (chanan); Greek χάρις (charis). Record noun, adjective, and verb separately.

**Default explanation:** A source discussing chen as a word family must not cause the adjective channun in Exodus 34:6 to be mislabeled as that noun. Grace does not lexically mean every later definition of unmerited favor in every context.

**Translation key:** Use favor, grace, gracious, or related wording according to grammar and relationship. Explain theological implications through the passage's argument.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 6:8 speaks of Noah finding favor. Exodus 34:6 uses the adjective channun in the description of Yahweh. Ephesians 2:8-10 relates grace to salvation, faith, and good works.

#### Local reading

Grace vocabulary includes favor, gracious action, and the effects or response associated with a gift. Hebrew chen is a noun, channun an adjective, and chanan a verb. Exodus 34:6 uses channun in God's self-description; it must not be tagged as an occurrence of the noun chen merely because the words are related. That distinction matters for exact-form retrieval and for an interlinear that represents what is actually on the page.

Genesis 6:8 describes Noah finding favor in Yahweh's eyes in a narrative of widespread corruption and impending judgment. The phrase identifies favorable regard; the wider narrative supplies its moral and saving setting. Genesis 33:8–11 uses favor language in a human relationship, showing why the noun's basic range cannot be reduced to a technical doctrine of conversion.

Ephesians 2:8–10 makes grace central to salvation as gift and excludes boasting. Romans 4:4–5 contrasts grace with a wage owed. In these contexts, freeness is strongly supported by the argument. Titus 2:11–14 also presents grace as teaching a changed way of life. Romans 6:1–2 rejects using grace as permission to continue in sin. These passages provide the theological account through their assertions; a lexicon cannot make the ethical teaching disappear by glossing the noun as favor.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use grace, favor, gracious, or show favor according to the form and setting. Dutch *genade*, *gunst*, and *genadig* are useful distinctions. If grace is a familiar church word but obscure to the reader, explain the particular generous action or favorable regard rather than adding an entire doctrinal definition inside the translation.

The default explanation is that God's saving grace is freely given in the relevant New Testament arguments and produces a new orientation of life. “Unmerited favor” summarizes an important theological claim in those contexts, but it is not the exhaustive meaning of every charis or chen occurrence. Charis can also concern gratitude or a gracious quality in other constructions. The actual source record must remain open to those uses.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The useful trajectory connects God's gracious character, favor shown in the saving narrative, and salvation and renewed conduct in Christ. Exodus 34 and Ephesians 2 can illuminate that trajectory, but their forms and contexts remain distinct. A project explanation should not count the adjective in Exodus as evidence for the noun's occurrence frequency.

The working synthesis joins gift and transformation without turning transformation into a wage that precedes the gift. Theology review asks whether a claim identifies salvation's source, reception, and intended result accurately. Good works in Ephesians 2:10 belong to the argument and must not be omitted from a grace study; verses 8–9 also must not be softened into a generic encouragement to try harder. Keep the grace, works, faith, and sanctification records linked for these specific comparisons.

### Full-depth semantic audit: grace, favour and graciousness

**Forms and core ranges:** Hebrew **חֵן (*ḥen*)** denotes favour, gracious regard or attractiveness in some social settings; **חָנַן (*ḥanan*)** is a verb of showing favour or being gracious; **חַנּוּן (*ḥannun*)** is a gracious adjective. Greek **χάρις (*charis*)** ranges across favour, generosity/grace, gift-related beneficence and in certain constructions gratitude or thanks. These are related but morphologically distinct and not interchangeable at each occurrence.

**Contextual controls.** Gen 6:8 says Noah found *ḥen* in Yahweh's eyes within the judgment narrative; God is the one who shows favour, but the phrase does not itself encode a complete doctrine of predestination. Gen 33:8–11 uses favour language between humans, showing that its ordinary range is not exclusively salvific. Exod 34:6 calls Yahweh **ḥannun**, not the noun *ḥen*, within a string also containing compassion and *ḥesed*: these terms illuminate one another without becoming synonyms. Eph 2:8–10 portrays salvation as gift excluding boastful earning, with good works explicitly given their proper place as intended result. Rom 4:4–5 uses wages as a foil for unearned gift. Titus 2:11–14 personifies grace as teaching changed conduct.

**Mercy and hesed boundary.** Mercy (*eleos*, barmhartigheid/ontferming) often emphasizes aid to a distressed party; *hesed* can emphasize beneficent love or fidelity; *charis/ḥen* often favour or giving, but the overlap is real. “Unmerited favour” is a useful *theological* explanation in specified saving contexts, not an exhaustive dictionary meaning of every *charis*. **Limit:** civil/social gratitude and charm uses require additional verified examples before claiming a complete corpus map.
