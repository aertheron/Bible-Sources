# WT047 — Kingdom, presence, temple, abiding

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** koninkrijk, aanwezigheid, tempel, blijven.

**Original terms:** Hebrew מַלְכוּת (malkut), פָּנִים (panim); Greek βασιλεία (basileia), ναός (naos), μένω (menō). A presence/kingdom hub links separate term records.

**Default explanation:** Shared presence themes do not make naos, panim, basileia, and menō equivalent words. A thematic connection needs a textual explanation.

**Translation key:** Choose reign/kingdom and sanctuary/temple from each use. Explain face/presence constructions idiomatically; do not impose one spatial definition on every kingdom reference.

**Initial orientation:** Exodus 25:8 concerns God dwelling among Israel. Psalm 16 speaks of joy before God. Mark 1 announces God's kingdom. First Corinthians 3 uses temple language for the community. Revelation 21 brings dwelling language into new creation.

#### Local reading

Kingdom, presence, temple, and abiding form a thematic hub with separate lexical records. Malkut and basileia can concern royal authority, reign, or a realm. Panim ordinarily concerns face and can express presence in particular constructions. Naos and hieron contribute distinguishable temple language; menō concerns remaining or abiding. None is a universal synonym for the others.

Exodus 25:8 concerns a sanctuary and God's dwelling among the people. Exodus 33:12–17 places presence language within Moses' request concerning God's accompanying Israel. Psalm 16:11 uses God's face or presence in a poetic account of life and joy. Each contributes to a presence trajectory, but the spatial idiom should not be expanded into an exhaustive theory of how God occupies physical space.

Mark 1:15 announces the nearness of God's kingdom in connection with fulfilled time, repentance, and good news. Basileia here cannot be reduced to a location where souls go after death. Luke 17:20–21 raises a debated expression translated within you or among you in an exchange with Pharisees. Its audience and Jesus' argument matter; an English preference for inward spirituality is not enough to settle the construction.

First Corinthians 3:16 addresses the community collectively as God's temple, using plural address with temple language. Individual bodily application in other passages should not erase that communal referent. Ephesians 2:19–22 similarly describes a people built together as God's dwelling. The grammar is important evidence against treating every temple statement as only a private inner experience.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use reign, kingdom, presence, sanctuary, temple, or remain as the particular construction warrants. Dutch *koninkrijk*, *heerschappij*, *aanwezigheid*, *heiligdom*, and *blijven* should retain these distinctions. Explain naos versus the broader temple setting represented by hieron where the distinction affects a narrative. Do not assume their senses are wholly non-overlapping in every ancient use.

John 1:14 uses dwelling vocabulary from skēnoō, not menō; the tabernacle resonance belongs in a contextual note. John 15:1–8 uses menō within a vine-and-branches image whose demands and effects are stated in the paragraph. “Abiding” is not a secret technical technique inferred from the verb alone.

The assembly subrecord for ekklēsia concerns an assembly or community in its actual setting. Its historical components do not make “the called-out ones” an exhaustive lexical definition or prove that every assembly is a church. Retrieve the church sense when the referent supports it and distinguish ordinary assembly uses.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A focused trajectory connects divine dwelling, Christ's presence, the Spirit-indwelt community, and Revelation 21:1–4's renewed dwelling with humanity. First Peter 2:4–10 adds a living-stones and priestly-community account. These are substantive links, not proof that each temple or presence word names the identical institution.

The working synthesis relates God's reign and presence to a people and a renewed world while preserving the current unit's referents. Theology review asks what is present already, what remains promised, and how the community's conduct expresses its vocation. Shared presence imagery must not automatically support a complete account of church polity, political sovereignty, or an eschatological timetable.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Trace dwelling and rule through sanctuary, Jesus, the Spirit and community, and new creation using the bounded canonical map.
