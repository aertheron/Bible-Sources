# WT042 — Word, speech, message, Logos

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** woord, spreken, boodschap, Logos.

**Original terms:** Hebrew דָּבָר (davar), דָּבַר (davar, verb); Greek λόγος (logos), ῥῆμα (rhēma). Preserve separate lexical records and constructions.

**Default explanation:** Every logos is not Christ as the Word, and logos/rhēma do not carry an automatic written-versus-spoken revelation distinction.

**Translation key:** Use word, statement, message, matter, or retained Logos from the actual setting. Identify whether the term refers to a person or to speech/content.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 15:1 introduces the word of Yahweh to Abram. Psalm 33:6 links God's word and breath with creation. John 1 makes the Word the prologue's referent. John 10 includes ordinary word/scripture language in an argument.

#### Local reading

Davar can concern a word, statement, matter, or event, while the related verb concerns speaking. Logos and rhēma have overlapping Greek uses involving word, saying, message, or discourse. Logos also has uses involving reason or account in wider settings. The particular clause determines what is meant. There is no reliable biblical rule that logos always means written Scripture while rhēma always means a private spoken revelation.

Genesis 15:1 presents the word of Yahweh coming to Abram in a vision. Psalm 33:6 relates God's word to creation, and Isaiah 55:10–11 presents divine speech as accomplishing its purpose. These provide a scriptural account of effective divine communication. They do not make every ordinary word occurrence a personal title or a direct reference to the incarnation.

John 1:1–18 uses Logos as a distinctive designation within the prologue's account of creation, life, revelation, and becoming flesh. Its opening echoes creation language; verses 1–3 assert relations that give the designation its theological force. The imperfect ēn presents the Word as being there at the beginning in the argument. The tense by itself does not mean beginningless existence: John 2:1 uses the same form for Jesus' mother being at a wedding. The prologue's total presentation, including the contrast with what came into existence through the Word, supports the stronger theological conclusion.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use word, message, saying, matter, or another warranted equivalent according to the referent. Preserve Word as a title in John's prologue, explaining its special use. Dutch *Woord*, *woord*, *boodschap*, and *zaak* may represent different local constructions. Capitalization helps the reader track interpretation; it is not independent lexical evidence.

The default explanation is that John's Word designation must be read through what the prologue says about its bearer. Logos alone does not mean “Jesus Christ” in every biblical occurrence. Similarly, the absence of an article before theos in John 1:1 does not automatically yield “a god.” Predicate syntax, word order, and the clause's relation to the surrounding statements matter. A grammatical note should explain the selected rendering without presenting one simplified article rule as decisive.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A focused trajectory connects creative and revealing speech with John's presentation of the Word made flesh. Possible wisdom-tradition associations can be explored where their details are relevant, but broad resemblance should not become an unmarked quotation claim. John 10:34–36 uses word language within a scriptural dispute and supplies a different local question.

Ephesians 6:17 and Romans 10:8–17 provide useful checks against the written/spoken distinction by placing rhēma in scriptural and proclaimed-message settings. The working synthesis treats God's revelation as effective and climactically disclosed in Christ, while keeping ordinary word senses intact. Theology review must distinguish tense and syntax observations from arguments about divine identity and eternal existence. A translation should preserve the text's claims before explaining their canonical significance.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Study creation, prophetic speech, and John's prologue without treating proposed echoes as an identical lexical use.
