# WT029 — Joy and rejoicing

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** vreugde, blijdschap, zich verheugen.

**Original terms:** Hebrew שִׂמְחָה (simchah), שָׂמַח (samach); Greek χαρά (chara), χαίρω (chairō).

**Default explanation:** Joy and sorrow can coexist. A word study must not turn a command to rejoice into a denial of grief or an unsupported psychological requirement.

**Translation key:** Translate joy or rejoice, explaining the cause and rhetorical setting. Do not insert a contrast with happiness that the passage does not make.

**Initial orientation:** Nehemiah 8 joins public reading, grief, and celebration. Luke 2 announces joy at Jesus' birth. Second Corinthians 6:10 places sorrow and rejoicing together. Philippians 4 links rejoicing with prayer and community conduct.

#### Local reading

Joy vocabulary concerns gladness or rejoicing, but its moral and theological significance depends on its object. Simchah and samach supply Hebrew noun and verb forms; chara and chairō supply Greek forms. A command to rejoice, a narrative report of gladness, and a greeting built from related language need separate contextual records. The shared family does not make them identical acts.

Nehemiah 8:9–12 presents people weeping as the instruction is read, followed by encouragement to celebrate, eat, drink, and share with those lacking provision. The joy of Yahweh is connected with the holy day and communal understanding; it is not a general prohibition against sadness. Psalm 30:1–5 uses a movement from weeping toward joy in thanksgiving for rescue. Habakkuk 3:17–19 deliberately places rejoicing in God beside severe loss of agricultural provision.

Luke 2:10 announces great joy in relation to the birth being proclaimed. Its grounds come from the message and the following identification of the child. Second Corinthians 6:10 describes sorrow and continuing rejoicing together within Paul's account of ministry. Philippians 4:4–7 encourages rejoicing in the Lord alongside prayer, gentleness, and the handling of anxiety. Those contexts resist equating joy with uninterrupted pleasure or denial of difficulty.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use joy, gladness, rejoice, or be glad as the grammatical form warrants. Dutch *vreugde* and *zich verheugen* preserve the noun/verb distinction. When a greeting is at issue, translate its social function rather than forcing the complete theological sense of rejoicing into it. A retained chara adds little unless the study actually needs the source record explained.

The default explanation is that joy can arise from God's action and coexist with grief, endurance, or need. Joy is not automatically holy merely because the same word is used in a biblical narrative; its object matters. Conversely, sadness does not by itself disprove a person's faith or erase the possibility of joy. Distinguish commanded communal practice from a demand to produce an immediate emotion on request.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A focused trajectory follows rescue, celebration, and the joy associated with God's saving announcement. A second concerns joy sustained amid suffering. John 16:20–22 describes sorrow becoming joy in Jesus' preparation of the disciples, supplying a specific narrative connection rather than a generic motivational promise.

The working synthesis locates joy in God's presence, action, and promised future while taking loss seriously. Theology review should ask what the passage gives as the reason for rejoicing and what practice follows. Nehemiah's sharing with those who lack food is a useful corrective to purely inward accounts of celebration. Do not transform a joy study into unsupported psychological or medical claims; the biblical material supports an account of faithful response, not a diagnostic measure of everyone's emotional health.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Follow celebration, promised restoration, and resurrection hope through selected contexts.
