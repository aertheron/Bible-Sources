# WT050 — Marriage, one flesh, desire, sexual ethics

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** huwelijk, één vlees, verlangen, seksuele ethiek.

**Original terms:** Hebrew בָּשָׂר אֶחָד (basar echad), נָאַף (naaf); Greek ἐπιθυμέω (epithymeō), ἐπιθυμία (epithymia), πορνεία (porneia), μοιχεία (moicheia), μαλακός (malakos), ἀρσενοκοίτης (arsenokoitēs). Each term needs a separate reviewed sense record.

**Default explanation:** Epithymia does not inherently mean sinful or covetous desire: Luke 22:15 is an essential comparison. A compound's roots, a later label, or one gloss cannot alone determine the scope of contested ethical vocabulary.

**Translation key:** Choose desire, covet, lust, adultery, or sexual-immorality wording from the actual construction and context. Retain porneia with a local gloss where a disputed English category would conceal the question.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 2 supplies one-flesh language. Exodus 20 prohibits coveting. Matthew 5 places desire within an adultery discussion. Luke 22:15 uses the desire family for Jesus' wish to eat Passover. First Corinthians 6 and First Thessalonians 4 supply distinct ethical arguments.

#### Local reading

Marriage, one flesh, desire, and sexual wrongdoing require distinct lexical and contextual records. Genesis 2:18–25 describes companionship, the woman's relation to the man, leaving, joining, and becoming one flesh. Matthew 19:3–12 uses the creation account in a dispute about divorce. First Corinthians 6:12–20 applies one-flesh language to sexual union with a prostitute while arguing that the body belongs to the Lord. The latter passage shows why the expression cannot simply be equated with a valid marriage contract in every occurrence.

Naaf and moicheia concern adultery in relevant Hebrew and Greek settings. Porneia is a wider sexual-wrongdoing term whose scope depends on context and contemporary usage. It can overlap with adultery and appears beside more specific terms in other lists. A study should not restrict it automatically to prostitution, derive every use from a sale metaphor, or pretend the word alone proves every modern boundary and comparative degree of guilt.

Epithymeō and epithymia concern desire or longing that can be good or wrongful. Luke 22:15 uses both the noun and verb for Jesus' desire to share Passover. Matthew 13:17 describes prophets' desire to see, and First Timothy 3:1 speaks of desiring a good work. These are decisive checks against the rule that the family inherently means covetous or possessive desire.

Matthew 5:27–30 instead supplies an adultery setting, intentional looking, and desire in relation to another person. The pros to construction expresses a purposive relation; it does not independently encode consuming, acquisition, or predation. A connection with the coveting command can be argued, but wrongful desire is established through this setting and construction, not by redefining every epithymeō occurrence as coveting.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use adultery where the specific offense is meant. Use sexual immorality or a qualified expression for porneia, retaining the Greek in a consequential scope note. Use desire, longing, lust, or covet according to the object and the passage's evaluation. Dutch *verlangen*, *begeerte*, and *begeren* can carry different moral implications; do not let a negative target-language convention decide the original word's full range.

Malakos ordinarily means soft, including clothing in Matthew 11:8. Its plural use in First Corinthians 6:9 concerns persons within a vice list and requires separate assessment; “soft clothing” is not the same sense. Arsenokoitēs in that list and First Timothy 1:9–11 plausibly relates to the Greek wording about male and bed in Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13. That relation is stronger evidence than merely splitting a compound, but the sparse usage still warrants explanation of scope. Preserve proposed male-male intercourse and narrower exploitation readings as assessable interpretations, with their evidential differences visible. Do not repeat the historical lexicon's “sodomite” label as an unexplained precise definition or equate an ancient conduct term with a modern orientation category.

The default explanation is that attraction, intentional cultivation of wrongful desire, adultery, exploitative conduct, and the wider porneia category are different questions. Their distinctions matter for moral proportion, while none becomes harmless merely because another offense is more severe. A lexical study is not a license to shame people through an imprecise category.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The focused trajectory connects created embodied relationship, covenant faithfulness, Jesus' treatment of marriage and desire, and holiness in communal conduct. First Thessalonians 4:3–8 explicitly joins sexual conduct with holiness, honor, and not wronging another. First Corinthians 7:1–9 and 7:32–35 also honor celibacy and address married and unmarried life; marriage should not be presented as the only dignified vocation.

The project's working account places sexual union within faithful marriage and emphasizes bodily dignity and moral proportion. Its ethical conclusions must be argued from the combined passages rather than derived from an inherently covetous definition of desire. Theology review should preserve the direct commands, explain contested lexical steps, and distinguish ancient textual claims from their application to present situations. A corrected word study can strengthen the argument by making its actual contextual support visible.

For unmarried and remarriage questions, preserve the actual forms. First Corinthians 7:11 uses agamos of a woman separated from her husband and instructs her to remain unmarried or be reconciled. First Corinthians 7:27–28 does not use agamos: verse 27 contrasts being bound to a wife with being free from a wife, and verse 28 discusses marrying. A conclusion about permitted remarriage requires explaining those descriptions and their relationship to the chapter's other instructions. It cannot be established by claiming the same unmarried adjective occurs in all these verses.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Keep Mark's covenantal argument attributed and assess each term and inference in its own literary, cultural, and canonical setting; explain supported alternatives respectfully.
