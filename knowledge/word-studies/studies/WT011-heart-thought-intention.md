# WT011 — Heart, thought, intention

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** hart, denken, bedoeling.

**Original terms:** Hebrew לֵב (lev), לֵבָב (levav); Greek καρδία (kardia). Related mind vocabulary such as νοῦς (nous) needs separate records.

**Default explanation:** Biblical heart language is not confined to feelings, nor is it simply a premodern name for the brain in every passage.

**Translation key:** Keep heart where the image works, explaining thought, desire, resolve, or intention as the context requires. Do not silently replace the metaphor with emotions.

**Initial orientation:** Deuteronomy 6 addresses whole-person love. First Kings 3:9 connects the heart with hearing and discerning in government. Mark 7 locates evil intentions within the heart. Romans 10 joins believing in the heart and confessing with the mouth.

#### Local reading

Biblical heart language frequently concerns thinking, choosing, understanding, and desire as well as emotion. Lev, levav, and kardia must therefore be interpreted through what the subject does with the heart. Deuteronomy 6:4–5 addresses wholehearted love and loyalty. First Kings 3:9 asks for a hearing heart to govern and discern. An explanation that substitutes “feelings” in both settings would hide the second passage's concern with judgment and understanding.

Mark 7:20–23 locates evil thoughts and a range of destructive acts in the human heart. Its contrast concerns the source of defilement in Jesus' argument, rather than the claim that bodies and external actions never matter. Romans 10:8–10 connects believing in the heart and confession with the mouth. Their pairing describes a response involving inward trust and public speech; it is not an anatomical account of two independent salvation mechanisms.

Jeremiah 31:31–34 and Ezekiel 36:25–27 place changed inner disposition within promised covenant renewal. A new heart is more than a passing emotion. The image concerns a changed capacity or orientation for knowing and responding to God. Romans 12:1–2 employs mind vocabulary for renewal and discernment, showing thematic overlap without making nous and kardia the same lemma. Different words can contribute to a coherent account of renewed persons.

#### Translation and default explanation

Retain “heart” or Dutch *hart* where the metaphor remains intelligible, adding “thought and intention,” “understanding,” or another supported explanation when needed. Replacing every heart with “mind” would lose the source image and some of its breadth. Conversely, treating heart and mind as an absolute emotional-versus-rational opposition misrepresents their overlapping usage.

The default explanation is that the heart often describes the inner center of a person's understanding, desires, decisions, and moral orientation. The local activity selects the emphasis. Second Corinthians 9:7, for example, concerns deciding in the heart about giving. That use is not exhausted by emotion. Physical-heart and spatial uses of the Hebrew vocabulary also exist; do not attach the full inner-person account to every occurrence merely because the same lexical family is present.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A useful trajectory follows covenant loyalty, the diagnosis of inward failure, promised renewal, and Spirit-enabled response. Start with the passage's actual concern: hearing and judgment in Solomon's request, defilement in Mark, or faith and confession in Romans. Only then select the relevant prophetic bridge.

The working synthesis treats thinking, willing, feeling, and acting as interconnected dimensions of the person. It should not turn biblical imagery into a modern theory of neuroscience or a fixed division of spiritual faculties. Theology review asks whether a proposed application honors the passage's concrete response. “Follow your heart” is not automatically biblical guidance: Mark's diagnosis and the promise of a new heart show why the heart's orientation matters. Cross-link soul, Spirit, and listening while keeping each word's evidence distinct.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Follow inner disposition, covenant loyalty, the promised new heart, and confession without treating all passages as anatomical descriptions.

### Full-depth semantic audit: heart, inner person and thought

Hebrew **לֵב / לֵבָב** (*lev/levav*) can refer to inner attention, understanding, deliberation, planning, remembering, desire, emotion, conscience-like moral evaluation and resolve, depending on clause. Greek **καρδία** (*kardia*) has overlapping inner-person uses; **νοῦς** (*nous*, mind/understanding) is related conceptually, not an interchangeable form. A literal bodily heart in an anatomical narrative would be a distinct concrete context; do not assert that every metaphor carries all mental and emotional functions at once.

**Participants and contexts.** Deut 6:4–7 commands love of Yahweh with the whole heart in a covenant summons: whole-person allegiance is at issue, not solely emotion. 1 Kgs 3:9 asks for a “hearing heart” capable of discerning just administration; Solomon is the requesting ruler and his people are affected. Mark 7:20–23 locates evil intentions and actions within the human heart; here moral causation, not mere abstract knowledge, is foregrounded. Rom 10:9–10 links heart-belief and mouth-confession in a salvation argument, not two separate metaphysical compartments.

**Lexical guardrail.** Do not move Hebrew *lev*'s ordinary thought and intention uses onto *ruach* just because both are inwardly related to human life. Neither “heart = feelings only” nor “heart = analytical brain only” represents the contextual range. Canonically the heart is the addressed seat of understanding, devotion, desire and responsible action; renewed-heart promises develop a theology of transformation, not a new lexicon. **Limit:** organ imagery, emotional idioms and cognitive causatives need independently inspected examples for exhaustive classification.
