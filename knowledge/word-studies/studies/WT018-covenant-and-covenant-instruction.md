# WT018 — Covenant and covenant instruction

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** verbond, verbondsinstructie, Torah.

**Original terms:** Hebrew בְּרִית (berit), תּוֹרָה (torah); Greek διαθήκη (diathēkē), νόμος (nomos). Covenant and instruction are related subjects, not synonyms.

**Default explanation:** Berit and diathēkē are not universally interchangeable merely because the latter often renders the former. Covenant relationship does not erase commands or promises.

**Translation key:** Use covenant or, where the setting requires, testament/will for diathēkē. Explain Torah as instruction in its setting instead of assuming every law reference means one identical legal system.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 15 concerns promise and covenant enactment. Exodus 24 concerns ratification at Sinai. Jeremiah 31 announces a new covenant and inward instruction. Hebrews 8 quotes that announcement in its argument about Jesus' ministry.

#### Local reading

Berit concerns covenant, agreement, or a binding relational arrangement, while torah concerns instruction or law. Their frequent connection does not make them synonyms. Genesis 15:1–18 places covenant-making within a promise to Abraham and a symbolic rite. Exodus 24:1–8 includes words, communal assent, and blood in the establishment of a covenant relation. The particular promises, commitments, and participants are necessary to explain each scene.

Torah may identify an instruction, priestly teaching, or a larger body of authoritative instruction. Calling it “instruction” can correct the impression that every occurrence means a modern statute book, but it must not weaken the binding force of commands where the text gives them. Psalm 1:1–3 depicts meditation on Yahweh's torah as a formative way of life, rather than the opposition of all law to all grace.

Jeremiah 31:31–34 promises a new covenant with Israel and Judah, contrasting it with the covenant broken by their ancestors and describing instruction written within, knowledge of God, and forgiveness. Hebrews 8:6–13 cites that promise in its argument about Christ's mediation. The quoted identification of the parties remains part of the source context even when a Christian account discusses wider participation. Hebrews 9:15–22 raises further questions because diathēkē can function in covenant and testament reasoning; the argument cannot be settled by insisting that Greek usage has only one permissible sense.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use covenant for berit in the relevant biblical arrangements, with enough explanation to identify the relation. Retain Torah with an instruction/law gloss where useful. For nomos, identify whether the author means Mosaic law, a scriptural collection, a principle, or another local use. Do not replace every nomos with Torah when the construction supports a different sense.

The default explanation is that covenant combines relationship with specified commitments, promises, and responsibilities. “Relationship” does not mean an arrangement without commands, and “law” does not mean an impersonal system disconnected from God's people. A theological contrast between covenants must come from their actual provisions and the author's argument.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The useful trajectory follows promise, covenant formation, covenant failure, and promised renewal through Christ. Luke 22:19–20 connects Jesus' blood with new-covenant language. Matthew 5:17–20 adds an important challenge to explanations that describe fulfillment as simple dismissal of the earlier Scriptures.

The working synthesis holds continuity of God's purpose together with changes asserted in the new-covenant texts. It should specify which commands, institutions, or promises the passage discusses rather than announce that everything is either unchanged or abolished. Theology review asks whether the explanation accounts for mediation, forgiveness, inward renewal, and the relationship between earlier and later textual settings. Keep covenant and instruction linked while retaining distinct lexical records and retrieval paths.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Keep covenant parties, promises, obligations, signs, and fulfillment distinct while tracing development.

### Full-depth semantic audit: covenant and instruction are not synonyms

Hebrew **בְּרִית (*berit*)** designates an agreement/covenant or binding relational arrangement; **תּוֹרָה (*torah*)** can mean instruction, teaching or law depending on object and setting. Greek **διαθήκη (*diathēkē*)** frequently represents covenant but can be pressed into testament/will-related reasoning in relevant contexts; **νόμος (*nomos*)** can be Mosaic law, a particular command, scriptural collection or more general ordering/standard. Neither a shared English term nor their presence in a theological scheme makes them lexically identical.

**Parties and obligations.** Gen 15 involves Yahweh's promise to Abraham and covenant enactment; Exod 24 features Moses, Israel, covenant words, assent and blood. Ps 1's delight in *torah* names formative instruction, not a denial of binding commands. Jer 31:31–34 explicitly identifies Israel and Judah, a broken former covenant, internalized instruction, knowledge of God and forgiveness. Heb 8 quotes and argues from that passage; a Christian account of participation must preserve the parties named in Jeremiah even while examining Christ's mediating role.

**Difficult Greek construction.** Heb 9:15–22 plays on *diathēkē* and questions of death, covenant and testamentary force. No universal gloss “covenant only” or “will only” can decide its logic without sentence-level exegesis. **Theological synthesis:** promise, faithful response, instruction, covenant breach and promised renewal are real canonical stages, not new lexical senses of *berit*. “Torah means only loving teaching and never law” is as reductive as “Torah is merely a modern statutory code.” **Limit:** this is a paired conceptual hub; its source words need independent semantic inventories in particular ancient legal genres.
