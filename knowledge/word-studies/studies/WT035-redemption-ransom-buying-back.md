# WT035 — Redemption, ransom, buying back

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** verlossing, losprijs, vrijkopen.

**Original terms:** Hebrew גָּאַל (gaal), פָּדָה (padah); Greek λύτρον (lytron), ἀπολύτρωσις (apolytrōsis), ἀγοράζω (agorazō). Several social and theological images meet here.

**Default explanation:** A ransom image alone does not identify an unstated payee or settle a full theory of atonement. Different redemption settings are not identical transactions.

**Translation key:** Retain redeem/ransom where useful and explain the image's concrete setting. State which parties and actions the passage actually identifies.

**Initial orientation:** Exodus 6 announces deliverance from slavery. Leviticus 25 concerns recovery of property through a family redeemer. Mark 10:45 uses ransom in Jesus' service-and-death saying. Ephesians 1 relates redemption and forgiveness.

#### Local reading

Redemption vocabulary draws on rescue, ransom, purchase, and kinship responsibilities. Gaal can concern acting as a kinsman-redeemer; padah often concerns ransom or release. Greek lytron names ransom, apolytrōsis redemption or deliverance, and agorazō buying. These images can interact in theology while describing different actual relations and actions.

Leviticus 25:25 concerns recovery of a relative's sold property within land and family provisions. Ruth 4:1–12 combines kinship, land, marriage, and the preservation of a family line in a narrative transaction. The two settings should not be equated as though they were one universally identical legal procedure. The story gives its own participants, conditions, and public witnesses.

Exodus 6:6 uses redeeming language for Yahweh's deliverance of Israel through mighty action. Deuteronomy 7:7–9 relates release from slavery to God's love and oath. These passages give redemption a saving and covenantal weight without necessarily describing an ordinary cash payment to an identified purchaser. The action and outcome must define how the social image is being used.

Mark 10:45 describes the Son of Man giving his life as a ransom for many, within teaching that contrasts domination with service. The relation of the self-gift to the beneficiaries is explicit; a recipient of a commercial payment is not named. Ephesians 1:7 connects redemption with Christ's blood and forgiveness. Hebrews 9:12–15 gives another sacrificial and covenantal setting, requiring attention to its own argument.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use redeem, ransom, rescue, buy, or buy back where warranted. Do not insert “back” into every purchase expression unless the prior relation is relevant. Dutch *verlossen*, *loskopen*, and *vrijkopen* convey different emphases. A term key should explain the chosen image rather than turn every occurrence into a literal market transaction.

The default explanation is that redemption concerns costly rescue or restoration where the passage establishes those features. The social metaphor does not itself identify an unstated payee, such as Satan, or determine every element of an atonement theory. An interpreter proposing such a theory must supply evidence beyond the word ransom.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The focused trajectory connects liberation from slavery, restoration of persons or inheritance, and deliverance through Christ. First Corinthians 6:19–20 applies being bought with a price to the body's belonging and conduct, making ethics part of the image's use. It does not portray the person as an ethically disposable possession.

The working synthesis joins deliverance with restored belonging and responsibility. Theology review should distinguish the image's demonstrated features from details supplied by analogy. Price, cost, liberation, ownership, kinship, and covenant are not all equally explicit in every occurrence. Explain only the relevant relations for the user's passage, linking salvation, forgiveness, servant, and inheritance entries selectively. This preserves the strength of the rescue image while avoiding an unsupported account of invisible financial mechanics.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Connect family restoration, exodus, and Christ's work with each image's limits visible.

### Full-depth semantic audit: redeemer, ransom, purchase

Hebrew **גָּאַל (*ga’al*)** can concern the action of a **kinsman-redeemer** restoring a relative's property, family claims or person under specified social arrangements; **פָּדָה (*padah*)** can concern ransom or redeeming/setting free by intervention. Greek **λύτρον (*lytron*)** names ransom, **ἀπολύτρωσις (*apolytrōsis*)** deliverance/redemption, and **ἀγοράζω (*agorazō*)** buying or acquisition in ordinary and theological contexts. They are related images, not interchangeable transfer mechanics.

**Social and narrative roles.** Exod 6:6–8 announces Yahweh's deliverance of enslaved Israel from Egypt: the people and oppressor are specified; a market payment is not the focus of the narrative. Lev 25 sets out family property recovery with close kin, obligations and economic vulnerability; its kinship structure is essential before comparing Christ. In Ruth, a redeemer role is embedded in kinship and responsibility, but not every generous relative's act is automatically a completed statutory redemption. Mark 10:45 speaks of the Son of Man giving His life as a ransom for many in a larger context of service and status reversal. Eph 1:7 relates redemption and forgiveness through Christ.

**Atonement boundary.** The noun *lytron* supports a costly liberation image; it does not lexically identify exactly to whom a payment is made. “Bought” can be a metaphor of belonging and deliverance without a fully specified ancient commercial transaction in every verse. Canonically exodus, family redemption and Jesus' sacrificial service converge, while each contributes a different picture. **Limit:** complete legal go'el practices and Second Temple ransom history need separately sourced historical research.
