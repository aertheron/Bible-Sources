**Initial orientation:** Isaiah's servant passage includes suffering and offering language. Acts 3:13 calls Jesus God's servant. Romans 3:25 uses hilastērion within justification and redemption. Philippians 2 speaks of a slave/servant form. Revelation 5 depicts a slaughtered yet standing Lamb.

#### Local reading

Servant, sacrifice, lamb, and atonement language offers several images of vocation and saving action. Hebrew eved and Greek doulos can concern a servant or slave in different social relations. Pais can concern a child or servant. Context must choose the local application; a theological connection between filial and servant themes does not mean that both senses are simultaneously asserted by every occurrence.

Isaiah 41:8–10 identifies Israel as servant, while Isaiah 49:1–6 gives a servant a mission involving Israel and the nations. Isaiah 52:13–53:12 describes suffering, the bearing of others' wrongdoing, death, and vindication. Those distinctions matter for reading the servant material before tracing its Christian reception. Acts 3:13's designation of Jesus as God's pais has a strong servant setting; First Peter 2:21–25 explicitly uses the suffering-servant account.

Asham can concern guilt, liability, compensation, or a guilt offering according to its setting. Leviticus 5:14–19 gives a ritual and reparative setting; Isaiah 53:10 employs offering language in the servant passage. A substitutionary dimension can be argued from the relations asserted in Isaiah, but “the most precisely substitutionary sacrifice” is a comparative theological assessment, not the noun's dictionary meaning.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use servant or slave according to the actual social relation, explaining where softer English obscures bondage. Use child for pais where the context supports that referent, rather than treating servant and child as a fixed double gloss. Amnos and arnion should retain separate lamb records. The latter's diminutive formation does not make every Revelation occurrence an emphasis on a physically tiny animal; the visionary role and description control the interpretation.

Romans 3:25 uses hilastērion, a neuter form linked in lexical records with hilastērios. Hebrews 9:5 uses it for the atonement cover. Exodus 25:17–22 and Leviticus 16:14–19 establish the kapporet setting. In Romans, atonement-place, means of atonement, expiation, and propitiation are interpretive candidates requiring the context of blood, faith, God's righteousness, and prior sins. Record the chosen wording with its rationale and a substantive footnote; the isolated gloss “propitiation” cannot itself prove the whole argument.

The default explanation is that these passages portray Christ's self-giving through distinct servant and sacrificial images. Removal of sin, the addressing of guilt, divine judgment, and restored relationship must be established from their actual statements rather than assigned to one term as a complete atonement system.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The strongest bounded links are Isaiah's servant and explicit apostolic reception, and sanctuary atonement imagery with Hebrews or Romans. John 1:29 and Revelation 5:6–10 add lamb imagery, but Passover, the servant comparison, and other sacrificial backgrounds should not be collapsed into one rite.

The working synthesis holds the saving cost, the beneficiaries, and God's initiative together. Theology review asks which relations are explicit and which belong to a proposed model. An image alone does not identify an unstated commercial payee or specify every mechanism of punishment and transfer. Preserve the theological force of Christ's death while letting the user see how the text supports the account selected.
