# WT030 — Sin, failure, missing

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** zonde, falen, missen.

**Original terms:** Hebrew חָטָא (chata/khata), חַטָּאת (chattat); Greek ἁμαρτία (hamartia), ἁμαρτάνω (hamartanō). The noun can have different contextual uses, including offering language.

**Default explanation:** The illustration missing the mark does not establish that every sin is accidental, nor does a root image contain the entire biblical doctrine of sin.

**Translation key:** Choose miss, sin, or a context-specific offering expression from the actual use. Keep literal activity distinct from moral application.

**Initial orientation:** Judges 20:16 uses the verb for not missing with a sling. Genesis 4:7 personifies sin in Cain's crisis. Romans 3:23 concerns universal sin within Paul's argument about righteousness.

#### Local reading

Sin vocabulary includes wrongful action, guilt, and broader descriptions of a condition or ruling power. Chata can concern missing or failing in some settings. Judges 20:16 describes skilled sling users who do not miss, giving a concrete use that illustrates the root's range. It does not prove that every moral occurrence is an accidental mistake or that the entire doctrine of sin can be derived from a target-shooting image.

Genesis 4:7 personifies sin in Cain's setting through the image of something crouching and a summons concerning rule over it. The narrative's tension, warning, and subsequent violence determine the passage's moral force. Chattat can also concern a sin or purification offering in sacrificial contexts such as Leviticus 4. The object and ritual description must identify that use; an interlinear should not render every noun occurrence “sin” and leave an offering clause nonsensical.

Romans 3:23 asserts that all have sinned within the account of righteousness, redemption, and justification. Romans 5:12–21 and 6:12–14 develop sin language through reign, slavery, death, and changed allegiance. Here hamartia is not merely a countable list of bad acts. Romans 7:7–13 explores sin's relation to the commandment, adding an argumentative personification that requires reading the verbs and contrasts.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use sin, wrongdoing, or a more specific supported rendering; use sin offering or purification offering where the ritual context establishes that referent. A note can explain the range rather than pretending that one English word is adequate in every clause. Dutch *zonde*, *overtreding*, and offering terms likewise require local selection.

The default explanation is that “missing the mark” is an illustration of part of the word family's usage, not the exhaustive definition of every biblical sin. It cannot make deliberate rebellion accidental, erase responsibility, or supply the standard being violated without the passage's help. Paul's personified sin language also should not be flattened into an assertion about one isolated mistake.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The useful trajectory connects human failure and wrongdoing, their destructive consequences, and God's saving response through Christ. Romans 5 supplies a concrete Adam–Christ comparison; it should be explained from Paul's argument rather than from the etymology of hamartia. Related transgression and iniquity entries add distinctions without providing an inflexible taxonomy in which each biblical term always names one exclusive class.

The working synthesis treats sin as morally accountable failure in relation to God and others, with passages also describing its power and consequences. Theology review should ask whether an explanation preserves agency, harm, guilt, and the possibility of deliverance in the actual unit. Definitions such as “anything less than perfection” or “mere error” require qualification; neither can replace the text's varied descriptions of ignorance, intentional action, corruption, and grace.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Trace human vocation, failure, domination by sin, and restoration without replacing the authors' distinct images.
