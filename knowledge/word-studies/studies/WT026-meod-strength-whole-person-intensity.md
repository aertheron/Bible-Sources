# WT026 — Meod, strength, whole-person intensity

**Dutch aliases:** meod, kracht, zeer, geheel.

**Original terms:** Hebrew מְאֹד (meod); Greek ἰσχύς (ischys) and related strength vocabulary in relevant reception contexts. Verify each actual rendering.

**Default explanation:** Strength is a context-dependent rendering of this unusual construction, not the meaning to substitute wherever meod occurs.

**Translation key:** Translate the intensifier as very where appropriate. Explain all one's meod in the Shema instead of assuming a separate physical faculty.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 1:31 uses meod as an intensifier in very good. Deuteronomy 6:5 places it after heart and nephesh in the command to love. Mark 12 quotes and develops the command with Greek vocabulary.

#### Local reading

Meod is frequently an intensifier meaning very, greatly, or exceedingly. Genesis 1:31 describes what God has made as very good. Substituting “strength” for meod there would be inappropriate. Deuteronomy 6:5 uses it in an unusual nominal expression concerning loving Yahweh with all one's meod. This construction supports renderings such as might or strength, while the word's ordinary intensifying use remains relevant to explaining its force.

An illustrative rendering “with all your muchness” can help a learner notice the unusual Hebrew expression, but it is not natural working prose and should not masquerade as a settled exhaustive meaning. Interpretations involving all one's capacities or resources may illuminate the summons. They require an explanation of the phrase and its reception, not a claim that meod normally names wealth or muscular power.

Mark 12:28–34 places the command within a conversation about the greatest commandment and adds explicit mind language alongside heart, soul, and strength. Luke 10:25–28 also presents a fuller sequence. Second Kings 23:25 uses heart, nephesh, and meod language in describing Josiah's turning to Yahweh. These are useful reception and application contexts rather than evidence that every listed human faculty is a separate metaphysical component.

#### Translation and default explanation

For Deuteronomy 6:5, “with all your strength” or “with all your might” remains a reasonable working translation. Explain that the Hebrew employs a word commonly used to intensify, here substantivized in a whole-person summons. Dutch *met al je kracht* communicates the reception well; a study note can make the unusual source expression visible.

The default explanation is that the phrase calls for wholehearted commitment involving the person's available capacities. Strength is a contextual rendering here, not a universal gloss for meod. An interlinear should preserve the inflected form and the relationship of “all” to each expression, rather than producing an English faculty chart that the source does not supply.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The useful trajectory connects exclusive allegiance in the Shema with Jesus' affirmation of love for God and neighbor. In Mark 12, the scribe's response and the comparison with sacrifices further specify the force of the commands. The phrase's meaning is not exhausted by a physical-strength metaphor; its literary setting concerns covenant loyalty in thought and action.

The working synthesis interprets the sequence as a comprehensive summons rather than an inventory that divides the human being into competing parts. That reading should still preserve the individual words and the differences in their Greek reception. A theology review asks whether a proposal draws its account of worship and obedience from the dialogue rather than from a creatively expanded etymology.
