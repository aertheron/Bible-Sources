# WT037 — Witness, testimony, martyr

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** getuige, getuigenis, martelaar.

**Original terms:** Hebrew עֵד (ed); Greek μάρτυς (martys, often transliterated martus), μαρτυρία (martyria), μαρτυρέω (martyreō).

**Default explanation:** The later English martyr association must not make every martys mean a person killed for the faith.

**Translation key:** Use witness or testimony for the actual form; explain whether the context concerns observed events, a legal claim, public proclamation, or faithful witness under suffering.

**Initial orientation:** Deuteronomy 19 concerns legal witnesses. Isaiah 43 identifies Israel as witnesses. Acts 1 concerns witness to Jesus empowered by the Spirit. Revelation 1 calls Jesus the faithful witness.

#### Local reading

Witness vocabulary concerns testimony, evidence, and the person who bears it. Hebrew ed and Greek martys, martyria, and martyreō provide distinct noun and verb records. A witness may attest an event, support a legal claim, or testify to God's action. The English association between martyr and someone killed for faith reflects a developed usage and must not be imposed on every biblical witness occurrence.

Deuteronomy 19:15 gives a legal safeguard concerning the establishment of a matter by multiple witnesses. Numbers 35:30 addresses testimony in a capital case. These passages concern responsible evidence; they are not a numerical rule for deciding every theological interpretation or textual variant. Several manuscripts copied from one line also do not become independent historical witnesses merely because their number is larger.

Isaiah 43:10 calls God's people witnesses within the proclamation of Yahweh's uniqueness and saving action. Acts 1:8 connects witness to Jesus with the Spirit and a geographic movement. John 5:31–39 discusses testimony through several sources within a dispute over Jesus' claims. The content, agent, and evidential role make each use meaningful rather than the word alone proving that every testimony is true.

#### Translation and default explanation

Translate witness, testimony, testify, or bear witness according to the form. Dutch *getuige*, *getuigenis*, and *getuigen* preserve the distinction. “Martyr” is suitable when the context actually foregrounds faithful witness through death or the discussion concerns that later usage, but a note should make the transition visible.

The default explanation is that a martyr is a particular kind of witness in contexts where death is involved; biblical martys does not always mean a person who dies. Acts 22:20 identifies Stephen as Jesus' witness while referring to the shedding of his blood. Revelation 1:5 calls Jesus the faithful witness alongside firstborn from the dead and ruler of earthly kings. Those surrounding descriptions contribute the death and exaltation dimensions rather than placing them into every lexical occurrence.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A focused trajectory connects witness to God's acts, testimony to Jesus' resurrection and authority, and faithfulness under opposition. Revelation 12:10–11 joins testimony with costly fidelity in its visionary account. The genre matters: its imagery should be explained before drawing direct historical applications.

The working synthesis treats testimony as both proclamation and accountable attestation where the passages establish those features. Theology review should distinguish the witness's claim, the text's evaluation of its reliability, and the response sought from the hearer. A false witness remains a witness in the lexical sense; the noun is not a truth guarantee. Nor should studying martyrdom romanticize harm or require every believer to reproduce one narrative's circumstances. The ordinary task of faithful testimony remains visible alongside its costly expressions.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Follow truth-bearing and faithful testimony while distinguishing legal, prophetic, and apostolic settings.

### Full-depth semantic audit: witnesses and testimony

Hebrew **עֵד (*‘ed*)** describes a witness who can attest events or testimony; Greek **μάρτυς (*martys*)**, **μαρτυρία (*martyria*)** and **μαρτυρέω (*martyreō*)** identify a witness, testimony and the act of witnessing. The English-derived term **martyr** acquired a strong association with dying for testimony, but the basic ancient Greek noun is not always “one who has been killed.” Whether a witness is reliable, lying, judicially competent or persecuted is contextual.

**Roles and audiences.** Deut 19:15–21 concerns legal procedures with witnesses and evaluation of false testimony: the court, accusation and affected defendant determine the force. Isa 43:10 addresses Israel as Yahweh's witnesses to His unique identity; this is more than a courtroom transcript but retains the logic of attestation. Acts 1:8 makes disciples witnesses of Jesus empowered by the Spirit, with geographical expansion and a public message. Rev 1:5 designates Jesus the **faithful witness**, a claim about His character and testimony; in Revelation's setting faithfulness can involve suffering, but the lexical noun does not itself require death.

**Related conceptual boundaries.** Legal eyewitness, witness-bearing proclamation, faithful covenant testimony and costly martyrdom form an interconnected canonical pattern. Do not equate every disciple's act of telling a story with unexamined “proof,” nor treat martyrdom as the sole sense of *martys*. The historical resurrection claim should be evaluated from the named witnesses and textual setting, not presumed true or false from the word alone. **Limit:** the distinct noun/verb forms and ancient legal evidence practices require deeper source-by-source review.
