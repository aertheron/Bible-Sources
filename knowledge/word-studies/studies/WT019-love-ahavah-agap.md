# WT019 — Love, ahavah, agapē

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** liefde, liefhebben.

**Original terms:** Hebrew אַהֲבָה (ahavah), אָהַב (ahav); Greek ἀγάπη (agapē), ἀγαπάω (agapaō), φιλέω (phileō). Separate senses and forms.

**Default explanation:** Agapē is not automatically a technical term for divine, morally perfect love, and agapaō/phileō should not receive an inflexible contrast in every occurrence.

**Translation key:** Translate love while identifying its object, relationships, and actions. Explain self-giving from the relevant descriptions rather than treating it as a meaning built into every agap- form.

**Initial orientation:** Deuteronomy and Leviticus concern love of God and neighbor. John 3:19 uses the love verb with darkness as its object. John 13 shapes love by Jesus' example. First Corinthians 13 describes love's conduct.

#### Local reading

Love vocabulary can express affection, commitment, preference, or practical concern according to its object and setting. Deuteronomy 6:4–5 concerns love of Yahweh within a summons to exclusive allegiance. Leviticus 19:18 places neighbor-love beside commands about hostility, revenge, and the treatment of others. These contexts establish the required orientation and conduct; the Hebrew root alone does not supply the whole ethical explanation.

Agapē and agapaō are important in Christian texts but are not automatic labels for morally perfect divine love. John 3:19 uses agapaō for people's loving darkness rather than light. The object and the surrounding judgment make that love wrongful. A translation theory that assigns every occurrence a uniquely selfless divine sense cannot account for this use without changing its own rule.

John 13:34–35 gives Jesus' love as the pattern for the disciples' love and as a public mark of discipleship. First Corinthians 13:1–7 explains love through contrasts and descriptions within a discussion of communal gifts. Its claims about patience, kindness, boasting, and harm come from the paragraph's predicates; they are not all dictionary content encoded in the noun agapē. First John 4:7–12 grounds the communal summons in God's action through the Son.

#### Translation and default explanation

“Love” is usually a sound starting point, with a note identifying the object and expression. Dutch *liefde* and *liefhebben* require the same local attention. Retaining agapē can be useful in a study, but it should not imply that English or Dutch lacks any meaningful equivalent.

The default explanation is that biblical writers give love a particular moral shape through God's character, commands, and actions. The word alone is not that entire shape. Phileō overlaps with loving language and can also describe kissing. John 11:3–5 uses both phileō and agapaō in the account of Jesus and Lazarus' household. John 21:15–19 alternates them while also varying other vocabulary. A proposed distinction may be discussed, but a rigid “divine love versus merely human friendship” contrast should not be presented as an established lexical rule.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A focused trajectory connects whole-person love of God, neighbor-love, Jesus' self-giving example, and renewed communal practice. Matthew 5:43–48 extends the ethical comparison to enemies, providing a concrete test of interpretations that reduce love to warmth toward one's own group.

The working synthesis sees love as central to faithful life, with its content specified by these texts. It does not treat any strong feeling or desire as morally valid simply because someone calls it love. Theology review should distinguish the source word's local use from the evaluation of its object and expression. Link loyal love, mercy, faith, and marriage where relevant; do not retrieve all four automatically for every occurrence of agapē.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Trace commanded love, divine love, and Christ's example without exporting every positive description into negative-object uses.
