# WT027 — Peace, shalom, well-being

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** vrede, shalom, welzijn.

**Original terms:** Hebrew שָׁלוֹם (shalom); Greek εἰρήνη (eirēnē). Record greeting, safety, restored relations, and other contextual uses separately.

**Default explanation:** Peace can be richer than the absence of fighting, but wholeness should not be loaded into every greeting as its full theological content.

**Translation key:** Use peace, well-being, safety, or a retained shalom with a gloss where the context benefits. Name the particular relationship or condition.

**Initial orientation:** Numbers 6 uses peace in a priestly blessing. Isaiah 9 relates peace to royal rule. Ephesians 2 concerns reconciliation of Jews and Gentiles and access to God.

#### Local reading

Shalom can concern peace, welfare, safety, soundness, or well-being depending on its setting. The word's range is broader than the absence of warfare, but its entire range is not active in every greeting. Greek eirēnē also concerns public peace, relationships, security, and peace in relation to God. A theological explanation should identify the threatened or restored relation rather than repeat “wholeness” without showing what it means in the passage.

Numbers 6:22–27 places peace within a priestly blessing concerning Yahweh's favor and presence with Israel. Isaiah 9:6–7 joins peace with just rule and enduring royal government. Jeremiah 6:13–15 denounces assurances of peace where there is no peace, linking the false reassurance with wrongdoing and a refusal to face shame. These settings make peace morally substantive; peaceful language alone is not evidence that conditions have become right.

Ephesians 2:11–22 concerns Gentiles and Jews, hostility, access to God, and a reconciled community in Christ. Christ being “our peace” is explained through the actions described in the paragraph. It is not a dictionary claim that eirēnē means Christ wherever the word occurs. Romans 5:1–2 locates peace with God within justification and access through Christ, giving a related but distinct argumentative setting.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use peace, well-being, welfare, or safety according to the actual subject and situation. Retain shalom where a study needs the Hebrew concept visible, with a short contextual gloss. Dutch *vrede* can be supplemented with *welzijn* or *veiligheid* when those aspects are foregrounded; do not turn every greeting into an expanded doctrinal paraphrase.

The default explanation is that biblical peace can include restored relations and life under God's good rule. It is neither merely a pleasant feeling nor a guarantee that conflict and danger are absent. John 14:27 and 16:33 speak of peace while preparing the disciples for distress and opposition. Their relation to suffering should remain visible rather than being treated as a contradiction erased through a new gloss.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A focused trajectory connects blessing, just kingship, and reconciliation in Christ. Another concerns peace amid adversity. Select the route that explains the user's unit. Isaiah's royal imagery and Ephesians' reconciled community can inform a canonical synthesis without becoming identical claims about one political arrangement.

The working synthesis joins peace with justice, truthful diagnosis, and reconciled relationship. A theology review should ask whose peace is described, what hostility has been addressed, and what dangers remain in the passage. Applications should not demand superficial harmony that conceals harm, nor promise uninterrupted emotional calm. Keep hope and judgment linked where they explain the particular conditions of peace, rather than loading every peace-related trajectory for a simple greeting.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Connect blessing, royal hope, and reconciliation while preserving each passage's contribution.

### Full-depth semantic audit: shalom and peace

Hebrew **שָׁלוֹם (*shalom*)** can describe well-being, welfare, safety, harmony, restored relations, peace after conflict, or the absence of war. Greek **εἰρήνη (*eirēnē*)** overlaps, but its contextual scope must be read independently. “Wholeness” can summarize aspects of shalom in a particular passage; it is not a compulsory dictionary replacement in every greeting or inquiry about somebody's welfare.

**Participants and contexts.** In a common peace/welfare greeting the speaker may simply ask whether someone is well, without invoking a complete eschatological state. Num 6:24–26 concludes the priestly blessing with Yahweh giving shalom, a divine gift to the addressed community within a covenant worship setting. Isa 9's anticipated royal figure connects peace with just rule rather than merely the cessation of conflict. In Eph 2:11–22 Christ makes peace between previously divided groups and creates a new shared access to the Father; the affected parties and wall of hostility clarify what “peace” concretely entails.

**Lexical distinctions.** Peace, righteousness, covenant blessing, reconciliation and salvation form related themes but have separate vocabulary and are not all encoded in *shalom*. The phrase “peace to you” can be ordinary politeness or, in special narrated contexts, assurance of restored relation. Canonically peace can develop from flourishing and protection into reconciled community and final new creation; this is theological synthesis, not a separate sense hidden in every token. **Limit:** the present selected passages do not exhaust peace as negotiated treaty, economic welfare, greeting, inward rest, or absence of war.
