# WT038 — Passover, Pesach, pascha

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Name and phrase. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** Pesach, Pascha, paasfeest.

**Original terms:** Hebrew פֶּסַח (pesach); Greek πάσχα (pascha). Distinguish festival, meal, and sacrificial reference in each use.

**Default explanation:** The word's historical setting and its Christological application are related but distinct. Competing etymological explanations require evidence and must not control the translation alone.

**Translation key:** Use Passover/Pesach with a short explanation; do not substitute the later Christian Easter holiday for every source pascha.

**Initial orientation:** Exodus 12 gives the deliverance setting and instructions. Luke 22 situates Jesus' meal within Passover. First Corinthians 5 applies Passover imagery within a community-discipline argument.

#### Local reading

Pesach and pascha can name the Passover festival, its meal, or the sacrificial animal by contextual extension. Exodus 12:1–14 describes household arrangements, an unblemished animal from sheep or goats, blood marking the houses, a meal, and judgment followed by deliverance. The animal should not automatically be described as a lamb where the verse explicitly allows a goat. The narrative supplies protection, remembrance, and the formation of a liberated people.

Exodus 12:21–27 connects the action with future explanation to children. Deuteronomy 16:1–8 presents observance in relation to the chosen place and remembrance of departure from Egypt. These settings are related but have different immediate emphases. A historical account of Passover practice should distinguish the textual periods rather than treat all later customs as present in the first night of the narrative.

Luke 22:14–20 places Jesus' meal and words within Passover preparation and connects his coming suffering with covenant language. First Corinthians 5:7–8 calls Christ the community's Passover and develops an unleavened-bread comparison in an argument about communal conduct. Its typology is explicit, but the ethical use is part of the paragraph and should not disappear beneath a discussion of sacrifice.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use Passover, Pesach, or a target-language equivalent, identifying whether the festival, meal, or victim is meant. Dutch *Pesach* or *Pascha* may avoid confusion with ordinary modern Easter terminology when the source refers to the Jewish festival. Explain the selected convention rather than changing it silently between passages.

The default explanation is that Passover commemorates Israel's deliverance in the exodus setting and receives explicit Christological application in the New Testament. The historical name's disputed derivation should not predetermine every explanation. “Pass over,” protect, or spare may be discussed in relation to Exodus' wording, but etymological uncertainty does not remove the narrative's clear concern with rescue from judgment.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The focused trajectory connects exodus, protected households and covenant remembrance, Jesus' self-giving, and the community's changed life. John 19:31–37 contributes scriptural fulfillment and the non-breaking of bones in the crucifixion account. A Passover connection is important, while a detailed reconciliation of Gospel chronology requires a separate study of the accounts and festival terminology; the word pascha alone cannot settle it.

The working synthesis treats Christological typology as a relation supported by explicit apostolic application and literary correspondences. It should distinguish Passover from the Day of Atonement and other sacrificial rites before relating them in a wider account of Christ's death. Theology review asks which features of the original event the later author actually uses: liberation, blood, communal identity, meal, or removal of leaven. Do not import all ritual features automatically or assume every lamb image has one exclusive source background.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Trace exodus remembrance and Jesus' death while preserving the specific imagery and purpose of later applications.
