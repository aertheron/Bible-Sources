# WT007 — Election, foreknowledge, predestination

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** verkiezing, voorkennis, voorbestemming.

**Original terms:** Hebrew בָּחַר (bachar); Greek ἐκλέγομαι (eklegomai), προορίζω (proorizō), πρόγνωσις (prognōsis), προγινώσκω (proginōskō). Each needs its own contextual record.

**Default explanation:** The prefix pre- alone does not decide individual versus corporate election, the basis of foreknowledge, or a theory of human freedom.

**Translation key:** State who is chosen, for what, and in what temporal or corporate setting. Preserve the distinction between choosing, knowing beforehand, and determining beforehand.

**Initial orientation:** Deuteronomy 7 concerns Israel's election and God's love and oath. Romans 8 places foreknowledge and predestination within suffering, conformity to the Son, and hope. Ephesians 1 presents election in Christ within an extended blessing.

#### Local reading

Election, foreknowledge, and predestination are connected theological questions expressed by different words. Bachar and eklegomai concern choosing; proginōskō concerns knowing beforehand; proorizō concerns determining beforehand. Their shared presence in a theological system does not make them synonyms. A useful study identifies the agent, object, purpose, temporal setting, and grammatical complements of each occurrence before asking how the claims fit together.

Deuteronomy 7:6–8 describes Israel's election in relation to God's love, oath, and liberation from slavery, excluding national greatness as its explanation. It concerns a people with a historical vocation. Romans 8:28–30 identifies the goal of predestination as conformity to the image of the Son, within an argument about suffering and hope. Ephesians 1:3–14 speaks of choosing in Christ, adoption, redemption, inheritance, and sealing within an extended blessing. Selecting one phrase without this purpose and setting can make election appear as an isolated mechanism.

Foreknowledge of persons may be interpreted as advance knowledge, prior relational regard, or prior choosing, depending on the passage and wider argument. The basic verb does not automatically mean “fore-love” wherever it occurs. Romans 11:1–5 adds Israel, a remnant, and grace to the discussion. Acts 4:27–28 concerns God's determination in relation to the opposition to Jesus; that occurrence is not itself a dictionary proof that every human decision is causally determined in the same way.

#### Translation and default explanation

Preserve “chosen,” “foreknown,” and “predestined” or “determined beforehand” as distinct expressions. Explain the stated goal: adoption or conformity to the Son, for example. Dutch *verkoren*, *vooraf gekend*, and *voorbestemd* require the same distinctions. Do not replace the original wording with a complete Calvinist, Arminian, corporate-election, or other account inside the working translation.

The default explanation is that these passages affirm God's initiative and purposeful saving work. Questions about individual and corporate election, the basis of foreknowledge, conditionality, and human response require further exegesis. A corporate grammatical setting does not automatically exclude individuals; an individual application does not automatically exclude corporate identity. “In Christ” must be read as part of the actual construction rather than as a slogan that settles every dispute.

#### Biblical and canonical development

Two useful trajectories are Israel's election for covenant purposes and the people formed in Christ, and the Son-shaped destiny of believers amid suffering. The runtime should select whichever explains the user's passage. Romans 9:6–18 is an important challenge for accounts that make election simply a reward for prior human achievement; it requires its own literary treatment rather than being mentioned and dismissed.

The working synthesis should state its preferred account and the evidence that supports it, while naming the strongest unresolved challenge. No general doctrine of freedom follows from a prefix or one gloss. Theology review checks that grace, vocation, promises, warnings, and human response have each been given their textual weight. Link the freedom entry, but do not merge the two questions into a single lexical record.
