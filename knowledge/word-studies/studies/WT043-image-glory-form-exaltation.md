# WT043 — Image, glory, form, exaltation

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** beeld, heerlijkheid, gestalte, verhoging.

**Original terms:** Hebrew צֶלֶם (tselem), כָּבוֹד (kavod); Greek εἰκών (eikōn), δόξα (doxa), μορφή (morphē), κενόω (kenoō), ὑπερυψόω (hyperypsoō). These are linked questions, not synonymous words.

**Default explanation:** An isolated form or image gloss cannot settle Christ's nature. Do not import later technical definitions without distinguishing them from lexical evidence.

**Translation key:** Preserve each term's own meaning; explain the self-emptying through the clause's actions. Kenōsis is a later discussion label, while Philippians 2:7 actually uses a verb.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 1 assigns image-bearing to humanity. Exodus 33 concerns Moses' request to see God's glory. Colossians 1 describes Christ as image within a creation argument. Philippians 2 moves through form, self-emptying, obedience, and exaltation.

#### Local reading

Image, glory, form, emptying, and exaltation belong to linked theological questions but are different words. Genesis 1:26–28 gives image language a human vocation involving representation and rule within creation. Exodus 33:18–23 concerns Moses' request for God's glory and the limits of the encounter. Neither tselem nor kavod independently means every later theological claim about identity, visibility, or divine substance.

Second Corinthians 4:4–6 identifies Christ as God's image and connects the knowledge of glory with his face. Colossians 1:15–20 places image and firstborn language within a description of creation through and for Christ, his priority, the sustaining of all things, and his relation to resurrection and reconciliation. “Firstborn” can concern chronological birth or privileged status in different contexts. Psalm 89:27 demonstrates a royal status use. Colossians' broader clauses are essential to explaining its particular application; neither “first created” nor “rank only” should be supplied merely from one gloss.

Philippians 2:6–11 uses morphē for God and for a servant, contrasts attitudes to equality, narrates self-humbling, and describes exaltation. Morphē can concern form or appearance and has specialized uses in some philosophical settings. It is not an automatic technical equivalent of a later term for essence. The parallel, the actions, and the larger exhortation matter more than an isolated definition.

#### Translation and default explanation

Keep image, glory, form, emptied, and exalted distinct. The source uses the verb ekenōsen in Philippians 2:7; kenōsis is a later convenient label for the question, not the noun appearing in that verse. The following participles explain the self-giving through taking servant form and becoming in human likeness. The clause does not name divine attributes as objects removed.

Harpagmos in verse 6 is difficult. “Something to exploit” is a defensible working interpretation when the contrast is understood as refusing self-advantage; other historical renderings concern grasping, a prize, or seizure. Note the alternative and the argumentative basis of the selection. A historical lexicon's preference for robbery or seizure cannot alone settle the current exegesis, just as a popular modern gloss cannot make the uncertainty disappear.

The default explanation is that the passage portrays self-giving rather than self-advancement, culminating in God's exaltation of Jesus. It does not say simply that Jesus discarded deity. That conclusion about the negative claim is grounded in what the text actually specifies, while a full incarnation account requires the wider canonical argument.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A focused trajectory connects human image-bearing, Christ's disclosure of God, and transformation into his likeness, as in Second Corinthians 3:18. Another connects humiliation and exaltation with Isaiah 45's universal homage in Philippians 2:9–11. These are separate selectable routes, not a requirement to retrieve every image or glory text.

The working synthesis gives weight to Christ's relation to creation and divine homage while preserving the Father's role and the passage's ethical purpose. Theology review should state which conclusions come from the full unit and which remain disputed at a word or construction. Retaining morphē or harpagmos in a note can help the reader inspect the issue; unexplained transliteration is not an adequate argument.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Trace image-bearing, divine disclosure, and Christ's vocation, identifying textual observation before synthesis.

### Full-depth semantic audit: image, glory and form

The separate terms in this hub must not be fused: Hebrew **צֶלֶם (*tselem*)**, image/representation; **כָּבוֹד (*kavod*)**, glory/honour/weighty presence in context; Greek **εἰκών (*eikōn*)**, image; **δόξα (*doxa*)**, honour/glory; **μορφή (*morphē*)**, form/condition; **κενόω (*kenoō*)**, make empty/empty oneself; **ὑπερυψόω (*hyperypsoō*)**, highly exalt. Their relations are contextual and literary, not alternate glosses for one noun.

**Agents and purposes.** Gen 1:26–28 ascribes God's image to humanity, male and female, with blessing and vocation to rule; it does not explicitly itemize intelligence or any one capacity as the image's exhaustive definition. Exod 33:18–23 describes Moses asking to see divine glory within the covenant crisis, with limits on his perception. Col 1:15 calls Christ the image of the invisible God in a unit about creation and reconciliation. Phil 2:5–11 describes Christ in God's form, taking the form of a servant, self-emptying and obedience, followed by exaltation: the actions and contrasts shape the meaning of *morphē* and *kenoō*. The debated term **ἁρπαγμός (*harpagmos*)** in Phil 2:6 remains a contextual interpretive problem; it cannot be settled solely by “grasped” or “exploited” as a dictionary verdict.

**Theological limits.** The Christian confession that Christ reveals God and restores human vocation is a canonical synthesis. It should preserve the relevant literary predicates rather than using “image” as a shortcut for all divine attributes, or *kenoō* as proof Jesus ceased to be divine. Distinguish honour/glory from a literal substance or visible glow when genre is metaphorical. **Limit:** iconography, ANE royal imagery and the disputed Greek idiom need sourced historical comparison.
