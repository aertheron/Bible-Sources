# WT010 — Spirit, wind, breath, and Spirit-language

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** geest, wind, adem, Heilige Geest.

**Original terms:** Hebrew רוּחַ (ruach), נְשָׁמָה (neshamah); Greek πνεῦμα (pneuma). Linked subrecords: παράκλητος (paraklētos), ἐκχέω (ekcheō), πίμπλημι (pimplēmi), μένω (menō); these are not definitions of pneuma.

**Default explanation:** A word's whole semantic range is not active in every occurrence. Paraklētos is not exclusively a designation of the Holy Spirit.

**Translation key:** Choose wind, breath, or spirit from each setting; distinguish a grammatical genitive from a complete doctrine of the Spirit. Explain outpouring, filling, abiding, and advocacy in their own subrecords.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 1:2 requires assessment of ruach elohim; Genesis 2:7 uses neshamah for breath. Exodus 14:21 concerns wind at the sea. John 3:8 exploits wind/Spirit language. First John 2:1 identifies Jesus as the paraklētos.

#### Local reading

Ruach and pneuma include wind, breath, spirit, and uses involving disposition or spiritual agents. Neshamah is another Hebrew breath word and must not be mislabeled as ruach. Genesis 2:7 uses breath language from neshamah, while Exodus 14:21 describes wind at the sea. Genesis 1:2's ruach elohim raises a translation question involving Spirit of God, wind from God, or a strong wind. The phrase's grammar and the creation scene must be considered; the root alone cannot select among them.

Job 33:4 associates God's Spirit and the Almighty's breath with life. Ezekiel 37:1–14 exploits the connections among wind, breath, and spirit in a vision about the restoration of Israel. A translation should preserve the literary movement and explain it, rather than insisting that the word has one identical sense at every point. John 3:8 similarly draws on wind and Spirit language. Wordplay can make more than one sense relevant without justifying the claim that every occurrence carries the entire semantic range.

The Holy Spirit's identity and activity require the statements made about the Spirit. Romans 8:9–16 concerns belonging to Christ, life, leading, and adoption. John 14:16–17 concerns another paraklētos and abiding with the disciples. First John 2:1 calls Jesus paraklētos in relation to the Father. Advocacy vocabulary therefore must not become an exclusive dictionary name for the Holy Spirit.

#### Translation and default explanation

Choose wind, breath, or spirit according to the clause, giving a note where a consequential alternative is supported. Capitalization is an interpretive aid: the ancient source text does not provide modern English capitalization to settle divine identity. Keep the actual source word and referent in the record.

Outpouring, filling, and abiding are distinct verbs used in Spirit contexts. The pouring metaphor in Acts 2:16–18 does not imply that the Spirit is a physical liquid. Filling vocabulary is also used with emotions or other contents; a full account of empowerment must come from the narrative. Abiding describes remaining and, in relevant Johannine contexts, sustained relationship. Hebrew nava and navi identify prophesying and a prophet; their inclusion here links speech and Spirit activity without turning them into definitions of ruach.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The focused trajectory connects God's life-giving action, promised renewal in Ezekiel 36:25–27, and Spirit-enabled life in Christ. Pentecost supplies a further explicit prophetic reception where relevant. Do not equate every creation wind with a fully articulated later pneumatology, or treat theological development as permission to erase the early setting.

The working synthesis grounds the Spirit's significance in God's activity, presence, and the relationships asserted in the selected texts. Personal agency, divine identity, and relations among Father, Son, and Spirit require their own canonical arguments. A theology review should preserve those arguments while distinguishing them from word meanings and the grammar of a possessive phrase.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Trace creation, prophecy, new covenant, and renewed life. Keep Spirit-language observations separate from conclusions about personal identity and relationships.

### Full-depth semantic audit: ruach and cognition

Hebrew רוּחַ (*ruach*) can denote **wind** (Exod 14:21), **breath/life-giving animation** (Job 33:4; Ezek 37), **human spirit or disposition** in applicable contexts, and **God's Spirit** (Ezek 36:27; Rom 8 for corresponding Greek). Genesis 1:2's רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים permits questions of “Spirit of God,” “wind of God,” and an intensive wind expression; genre and discourse, not a one-word substitution, must rank them. Ezek 37 and John 3:8 intentionally exploit wind/breath/Spirit associations, without converting every instance into a complete polysemy bundle.

**Neighboring concepts must remain distinct.** Genesis 2:7 specifically says נְשָׁמָה (*neshamah*, breath), not ruach. Hebrew לֵב/לֵבָב (*lev/levav*) regularly carries thinking, understanding, deliberation and intention. The Greek νοῦς (*nous*) is another cognitive term; it is not identical to πνεῦμα (*pneuma*). Statements that ruach is simply “mind/thoughts” need an inspected lexical entry or compelling specific clause; an author's spiritual-theological association does not create a dictionary sense. Human emotional disposition is not synonymous with cognitive faculty.

**Roles and theology.** John 14:16–17 describes a *paraklētos*, while 1 John 2:1 applies the same advocacy designation to Jesus. Acts 2's pouring language is metaphorical action, not a synonym for Spirit. The canonical development from creation and life, to Ezekiel's promised renewal, to Pentecost and Christ's people supplies theological understanding of personal divine activity; it cannot be imposed retrospectively on every meteorological wind. **Limit:** a full catalogue of human spirit metaphors and text-critical alternatives has not been compiled.
