# WT023 — Compassion and mercy

**Translation key:** Retain compassionate or merciful wording that fits the actual term; use bodily or parental imagery as explanation where the passage supplies it.

**Initial orientation:** Exodus 34 describes Yahweh as compassionate. Isaiah 49:15 uses maternal imagery to express God's remembrance. Luke 10 depicts compassion becoming practical aid in the Samaritan narrative.

#### Local reading

Compassion and mercy vocabulary expresses responsive concern, pity, or kindness, often toward someone in distress. Rachum is a Hebrew adjective; rachamim is a related noun form; eleos is Greek mercy language; splanchnizomai describes being moved with compassion. Their association does not make them fixed one-to-one equivalents. The passage must supply the agent, the need, and the response.

Exodus 34:6–7 places compassion within God's self-description after covenant failure. Psalm 103:8–14 explains divine compassion through patience, forgiveness, and the comparison of a father's concern for children, with humanity's frailty in view. These surrounding claims make the image concrete. Hosea 11:1–9 uses parent-child language in a painful account of Israel's response and God's announced restraint; its literary movement should not be reduced to a detached dictionary slogan.

Isaiah 49:15 explicitly compares God's remembrance with a mother's relation to her nursing child. A maternal connection can therefore be argued directly in this passage. It does not follow that every racham-related occurrence is a hidden womb metaphor. The lexical database distinguishes a womb record from compassion records even where base identifiers or word families overlap. Historical association and active contextual imagery are different kinds of evidence.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use compassionate, merciful, pity, or an action-oriented expression as the clause warrants. “Moved with compassion” can preserve the emotional movement of splanchnizomai while a note explains the bodily idiom if useful. A literal organ or womb gloss should not replace the understood sense in ordinary prose.

The default explanation is that mercy often involves concern translated into action, while the particular action must be read from the narrative. In Luke 10:25–37, the Samaritan's compassion leads to care, transport, and provision; the final question identifies the neighbor through doing mercy. Matthew 9:35–38 connects Jesus' compassion with distressed crowds and a summons concerning workers. These texts define concrete responses rather than mere sympathetic feeling.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A focused trajectory connects God's compassionate character with the mercy expected from those who know him. James 2:13–17 relates mercy, judgment, and the inadequacy of kind words without practical aid. That is a useful bridge to the faith-and-works question when the user's passage concerns treatment of the vulnerable.

The working synthesis treats mercy as morally serious and relationally concrete. It does not erase judgment or make mercy an emotional disposition with no action. A theology review asks whether the interpretation honors the need and response actually described, and whether its social or doctrinal application is supported by the passage. Do not import every maternal image into every mercy term; use Isaiah's explicit comparison where that particular explanation helps. Keep kindness, grace, and mercy linked without collapsing their lexical records.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Trace divine compassion and human mercy in concrete narrative and covenant settings.

### Full-depth semantic audit: compassion, pity, mercy

Hebrew **רַחוּם (*raḥum*)** is a compassionate/merciful adjective; **רַחֲמִים (*raḥamim*)** relates to mercy or compassion; Greek **ἔλεος (*eleos*)** can describe mercy, pity or practical kindness; **σπλαγχνίζομαι (*splanchnizomai*)** describes being moved with compassion. They are distinct words even when applied to the same event. The historical *raḥam* / womb association must not be projected as an active maternal image into every token without literary evidence.

**Agents and effects.** Exod 34:6 calls Yahweh compassionate **and** gracious **and** rich in *hesed*, so these are overlapping parts of His revealed character, not one fused lemma. Ps 103:8–14 attributes patient, parental compassion to God toward vulnerable humans; the father's comparison belongs to this psalm. Isa 49:15 **explicitly** compares God's remembrance to a nursing mother, giving a solid local basis for maternal imagery. In Luke 10:25–37 the Samaritan feels compassion and responds through treatment, transport and ongoing care; the wounded traveller is the concrete beneficiary, and neighbouring religious figures do not provide that help. Matt 9:35–38 depicts Jesus' compassion at distressed crowds, leading into His concern for labourers.

**Related ideas and limits.** “Mercy,” “grace,” “love” and “hesed” overlap in divine action but are not always interchangeable Greek/Hebrew word equivalents. Divine compassion does not mean indifference to judgment: James 2:13 discusses mercy and judgment together. **Limit:** bodily idiom, maternal metaphor and legal “show mercy” formulas require occurrence-level checks rather than universal word-root claims.
