# WT033 — Repentance, turning, change of response

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** bekering, berouw, omkeer.

**Original terms:** Hebrew שׁוּב (shuv), נָחַם (nacham); Greek μετανοέω (metanoeō), μετάνοια (metanoia), μεταμέλομαι (metamelomai). Distinct words can share an English repent gloss.

**Default explanation:** An English repent shared by two verses does not prove an identical source term or sense. Conversely, different terms can overlap, so word difference alone does not resolve a tension.

**Translation key:** Translate turn, repent, regret, relent, or change of response according to subject, action, and construction; distinguish human repentance from descriptions of divine response.

**Initial orientation:** Ezekiel 18 calls for turning from transgressions and discusses responsibility and life. Mark 1 joins repentance and belief in the kingdom announcement. Luke 15 places repentance within lost-and-found stories.

#### Local reading

Repentance language spans turning, regretting, relenting, changed purpose, and changed response. Hebrew shuv often means return or turn and does not always concern moral repentance. Nacham has uses involving regret, consolation, and relenting, with stem and construction important. Greek metanoeō, metanoia, and metamelomai overlap in some settings but must retain distinct records. Their roots do not independently decide whether a response is genuine or saving.

Ezekiel 18:30–32 summons turning from transgressions and receiving a changed orientation of life. Mark 1:14–15 joins repentance with believing the good news in Jesus' announcement of the kingdom. Luke 3:7–14 makes the response concrete through changed treatment of others, provision, and rejection of exploitation. Luke 15:1–10 places repentance within Jesus' response to criticism of his reception of sinners. These contexts show more than changing an isolated opinion.

Divine regret and relenting require special care. Genesis 6:6–7, Exodus 32:14, and First Samuel 15:10–11 describe God's response using nacham language. First Samuel 15:29 also denies a human-like relenting in the announced rejection of Saul. The same Hebrew family occurs in the tension; it cannot be resolved simply by claiming the English translations hide unrelated words. The local speech, declared purpose, and distinction from human unreliability deserve explanation.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use turn, return, repent, regret, relent, or comfort as the actual construction requires. Do not translate divine nacham as repentance from moral wrongdoing merely because “repent” occurs in an older English version. Conversely, replacing every divine use with a bland expression that removes the narrative's grief or announced change would conceal the source's force.

The default explanation is that repentance in moral summons involves a changed response to God and wrongdoing, often evidenced in conduct. “Only change your mind” is too narrow as a universal explanation of New Testament repentance. The opposite rule that metamelomai can mean only superficial remorse also overstates the distinction. Matthew 27:3–5 describes Judas' regret and subsequent acts; the narrative, not a root contrast alone, establishes what happens. Second Corinthians 7:8–10 explicitly distinguishes kinds of sorrow and their results.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A focused trajectory links prophetic turning, kingdom proclamation, forgiveness, and renewed practice. A separate divine-relenting comparison is useful only when the user's question concerns God's response or apparent contradiction. Do not merge those into one simplistic definition.

The working synthesis treats repentance as a consequential reorientation where the passage calls for it. Theology review must distinguish changes in human conduct, divine response within a narrative, and claims about God's faithfulness or unchanging character. A proposed reconciliation should explain the tension's actual wording and limitations rather than merely substitute a more comfortable gloss. Keep unresolved interpretive steps visible.

### Full-depth semantic audit: turning, reconsidering and repentance

Hebrew **שׁוּב (*shuv*)** can describe physical return, turning toward/away from a person, or turning from wrongdoing toward Yahweh. **נָחַם (*naḥam*)** can describe regret, consolation or relenting/reconsidering a stated course in context; these are not simply alternative words for human conversion. Greek **μετανοέω (*metanoeō*)** and **μετάνοια (*metanoia*)** concern repentance/change of mind and response; **μεταμέλομαι (*metamelomai*)** can concern regret or change of feeling/purpose. Treat nouns and verbs separately.

**Contexts and actors.** Ezek 18:21–32 summons an accountable human to turn from wrongdoing and live, with life and death central to the appeal. The verb for turning does not itself encode every emotional and behavioral component of repentance; the surrounding commands fill that out. Mark 1:14–15 combines repent and believe in response to the kingdom announcement. Luke 15's lost-and-found parables portray repentance in relational and communal narratives, but the sheep's physical return is not a one-word theological definition. Where God “relents” in the Hebrew Scriptures (e.g. context-sensitive *naḥam* clauses), the wording cannot be translated by importing human remorse for sin into God's character.

**Important counterexample.** 1 Sam 15 uses divine non-relenting and relenting formulations in an argument about Saul: check the speaker and what course/action is at issue before asserting a contradiction or a metaphysical rule from the verb. Regret, sorrow, conversion, renewed loyalty and changed conduct overlap without lexical identity. **Canonical synthesis** connects Yahweh's call, human answerability and gracious restoration, while keeping warnings real. **Limit:** a complete inventory of the disputed *naḥam* stem senses and all Greek repentance constructions needs separate lexical access.
