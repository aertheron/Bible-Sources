# WT017 — Creation, disorder, new creation

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** schepping, woest en leeg, nieuwe schepping.

**Original terms:** Hebrew בָּרָא (bara), תֹּהוּ (tohu), בֹּהוּ (bohu), the phrase tohu wabohu; Greek κτίζω (ktizō), κτίσις (ktisis), καινός (kainos).

**Default explanation:** Bara alone does not state every philosophical claim about creation, and the presence of a creation word does not settle chronology or a scientific model.

**Translation key:** Explain unordered, uninhabited, or waste/void imagery from each passage. Preserve wild and waste as a proposed explanatory rendering, not a universal definition.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 1:1-3 introduces creation and the earth's initial condition. Isaiah 45:18 contrasts an uninhabitable condition with habitation. Jeremiah 4:23 reuses creation language within judgment imagery. Second Corinthians 5:17 concerns new creation in Christ.

#### Local reading

Bara, tohu, and bohu describe different aspects of creation or its condition. Genesis 1:1–5 introduces God's creative action, the earth's initial state, darkness, waters, and the ordering of light. Bara in a creation use does not independently specify the material mechanism, the duration of the process, or a complete philosophical account of creation from nothing. The narrative's clauses must provide the explanation.

The Hebrew lexicon also distinguishes homonymous or related bara records; a shared Strong number is not sufficient to choose the creation sense. Tohu can concern emptiness, waste, confusion, or futility in different contexts. Bohu is associated with emptiness in the limited phrase settings where it occurs. “Formless and empty” is a useful working rendering for Genesis 1:2, but its English words should not become universal scientific definitions of the Hebrew terms.

Isaiah 45:18 concerns God's purpose in creating the earth for habitation. Jeremiah 4:19–28 evokes an undone creation within judgment imagery. Their relation to Genesis is worth explaining, but Jeremiah's vision does not establish an earlier chronological event between Genesis 1:1 and 1:2. Shared wording and reversed creation imagery support literary interpretation, not an automatic reconstruction of an otherwise unstated prehistoric catastrophe.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use create, waste, empty, or another warranted rendering from the actual construction. Retain tohu wabohu in a note when the phrase's interpretation matters. Distinguish the Hebrew verb's stem and context; do not count a homonym as another example of creating. For Greek ktisis, distinguish the act of creation from created reality and relevant institutional or collective uses.

The default explanation is that creation language affirms God as the agent and source of the world as described, while a word's meaning alone does not decide modern scientific models or every metaphysical claim. Romans 4:17 and Hebrews 11:3 contribute further statements about God's action and the world's origin; those statements should be read directly rather than claimed as meanings hidden inside bara.

#### Biblical and canonical development

Second Corinthians 5:17 links being in Christ with new creation in an argument about reconciliation. Revelation 21:1–5 depicts new heaven and earth and renewed divine dwelling. These passages give a creation-to-renewal trajectory, but the adjective kainos cannot alone prove how much physical continuity or replacement occurs. Its contrast with neos is useful as a tendency, not an absolute rule.

The working synthesis connects God's ordering and life-giving purpose with the renewal promised in Christ. A theology review should distinguish creation's goodness, judgment's undoing, and renewed creation rather than treating them as one timeless metaphor. Explain a supported literary link before making a historical or doctrinal inference. For Genesis 1:1–2:3, recognize the completed literary unit under the translation scope exception; do not expand into two full chapters.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Trace ordering, habitation, de-creation imagery, and renewal, distinguishing direct wording from a proposed theological pattern.
