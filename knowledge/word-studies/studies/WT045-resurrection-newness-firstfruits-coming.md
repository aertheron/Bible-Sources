# WT045 — Resurrection, newness, firstfruits, coming

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** opstanding, nieuwheid, eerstelingen, komst.

**Original terms:** Greek ἀνάστασις (anastasis), ζῳοποιέω (zōopoieō), ἀπαρχή (aparchē), καινός (kainos), παρουσία (parousia); Hebrew זֶרַע (zera) and Greek σπέρμα (sperma) belong in a distinct offspring/promise subrecord.

**Default explanation:** Do not treat every ordinary presence/arrival as the final coming, or deduce a whole resurrection timetable from one word.

**Translation key:** Explain raising, making alive, firstfruits, newness, and coming according to their separate clauses. A new-creation connection does not erase a passage's original restoration setting.

**Initial orientation:** Ezekiel 37 interprets the bones vision in relation to Israel's restoration. First Corinthians 15 concerns resurrection and Christ as firstfruits. Second Corinthians 5:17 concerns new creation. Revelation 21 depicts a renewed world; Matthew 24 supplies coming-language within an extended discourse.

#### Local reading

This hub connects several related questions while preserving separate lexical records. Anastasis commonly concerns resurrection in relevant New Testament settings, but also has an ordinary rising sense, as in Luke 2:34. Zōopoieō concerns making alive. Aparchē concerns firstfruits or an initial portion. Parousia concerns presence or arrival; Philippians 1:26 uses it for Paul's coming, so it cannot always mean Christ's final return.

Ezekiel 37:1–14 interprets its bones-and-breath vision in relation to Israel's restoration. Its resurrection imagery can contribute to a later canonical account, but the stated referent should remain visible. First Corinthians 15:20–28 identifies the risen Christ as firstfruits and develops the order and goal of resurrection. Verses 42–54 describe transformation and the putting on of incorruptibility and immortality. These are claims about God's giving life, not merely survival by an independently immortal soul.

Second Corinthians 5:17 and Revelation 21:1–5 describe new creation in distinct literary settings. Kainos often emphasizes newness of condition or quality, and neos often emphasizes recency or youth, but the contrast is not absolute. Hebrews 9:15 uses kainē for new covenant and 12:24 uses nea for the same broad covenant subject. The adjective alone therefore cannot prove either simple replacement or a complete physical continuity model for new creation.

#### Translation and default explanation

Translate resurrection, make alive, firstfruits, new, and coming/presence according to the particular form and object. Keep the harvest image of firstfruits with a brief explanation of its relation to the awaited whole. For parousia, identify whose arrival and which setting the author means before importing an eschatological timetable.

The offspring subrecord distinguishes Hebrew zera and Greek sperma from these resurrection terms. Both can be grammatically singular while functioning collectively. Galatians 3:16's Christological use belongs to Paul's argument; singular grammar alone does not mean every seed promise can refer to only one individual. Galatians 3:26–29 also includes those belonging to Christ within Abraham's offspring, giving an important contextual check.

Completion and necessity are separate subrecords. Hebrews 2:10 and 5:7–10 use teleioō in relation to the Son's suffering and completed vocation. Hebrews 4:15 checks against interpreting this as correction of earlier moral sinfulness. Luke 24:25–27 supplies a scriptural-fulfillment context for what was necessary. Dei elsewhere can express ordinary necessity or obligation; it does not encode divine decree in every occurrence. Related completion verbs must not be counted as the identical lemma solely because an English translation says finished.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A focused trajectory connects restored life, Christ's resurrection, and the promised resurrection and new creation. Another connects promise and offspring with Christ and those belonging to him. Use one or two routes suited to the user's question, not every subtopic in the hub.

The working synthesis locates future bodily life in God's action through Christ while preserving the particular horizons of vision, present renewal, and future consummation. Theology review should distinguish lexical observations from claims about chronology, continuity, or the intermediate state. Explain the explicit order in First Corinthians 15 before expanding it into a full end-times scheme, and retain uncertainties not decided by the selected unit.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Keep Israel's restoration, Christ's resurrection, believers' hope, and final renewal as related but differentiated stages.

### Full-depth semantic audit: resurrection, life, firstfruits, appearance

Greek **ἀνάστασις (*anastasis*)** means rising/resurrection in relevant contexts, **ζῳοποιέω (*zōopoieō*)** giving life/making alive, **ἀπαρχή (*aparchē*)** firstfruits or first portion, **καινός (*kainos*)** new in quality, and **παρουσία (*parousia*)** presence/arrival/coming. Hebrew **זֶרַע (*zera‘*)** and Greek **σπέρμα (*sperma*)** concern seed, offspring or descendants in specific constructions; they are a **separate promise family**, not definitions of resurrection.

**Narrative and metaphor controls.** Ezek 37:1–14 employs raising bones in a prophetic *vision explicitly interpreted as Israel's national restoration*; any typological link to personal resurrection must be argued additionally. 1 Cor 15:12–28 presents Christ's bodily resurrection as the beginning/firstfruits of those who belong to Him, giving the agricultural metaphor its sequence and corporate significance. 2 Cor 5:17 uses **new creation** in a Christ-and-reconciliation argument; this does not mean *kainos* always denotes replacement rather than renewal. Rev 21:1–5 depicts new heaven and new earth, with creation-scale renewal. Matt 24 uses *parousia* amid warnings and apocalyptic discourse; ordinary Greek *parousia* can also denote someone’s arrival/presence, so it is not a dedicated technical term for every end-time model.

**Conceptual boundaries.** Resurrection is not merely an immortal soul's survival, yet a claim about the intermediate state is a distinct question from resurrection's completion. Firstfruits implies a beginning of a larger harvest in the argued analogy but cannot mechanically settle the exact chronology of every eschatological text. The promised *offspring/seed* language is contextual and may designate an individual or a collective line; Galatians' argument must be read in its own rhetorical setting. **Limit:** no full lexicon census of every “coming,” “making alive,” or *zera‘* usage has been performed.
