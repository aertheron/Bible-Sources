# WT005 — Righteousness and justice

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** gerechtigheid, rechtvaardigheid, recht.

**Original terms:** Hebrew צֶדֶק (tsedeq), צְדָקָה (tsedaqah), מִשְׁפָּט (mishpat); Greek δικαιοσύνη (dikaiosynē), δίκαιος (dikaios). Related concepts require distinct lemma records.

**Default explanation:** The English division between private righteousness and public justice can obscure overlap, but overlap does not erase distinctions in the original vocabulary.

**Translation key:** Use righteousness, justice, right conduct, or a qualified explanation when the genre and argument warrant it. Do not mechanically treat all these lemmas as synonyms.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 18:19 joins righteousness and justice in Abraham's household vocation. Deuteronomy 16 concerns judges and fair judgment. Matthew 6 introduces acts performed before others. Romans 3 concerns God's righteousness and the justification of sinners.

#### Local reading

Righteousness and justice overlap in biblical usage, but several Hebrew and Greek words contribute distinct expressions. In Genesis 18:19, Abraham's household is to keep Yahweh's way by doing righteousness and justice. The setting concerns a community's conduct and the promised purposes of God. Deuteronomy 16:18–20 makes the public implications concrete: judges must not distort judgment, show partiality, or accept bribes. Justice is not confined to an inward religious disposition.

Mishpat can name a judgment, decision, rule, right, or established manner. Tsedeq and tsedaqah concern rightness or righteous conduct in varied relations. Their meanings must be selected from their actual constructions, not from an automatic division between public justice and private virtue. Isaiah 1:16–20 connects right conduct with defending the vulnerable while confronting wrongdoing. This contributes to theological synthesis because of the passage's argument, not because each noun contains an entire social ethic.

Matthew 6:1 in the supplied SBLGNT uses dikaiosynē before examples involving giving, prayer, and fasting. Its issue is righteousness practiced for human spectators and reward. Some textual traditions have a more specific almsgiving reading at the opening; an English difference here may therefore involve a textual variant, not merely two glosses for the same word. Romans 3:21–31 uses righteousness language in an argument about God's saving action and justification.

#### Translation and default explanation

“Righteousness” is useful when the author's argument depends on the related righteous/justify vocabulary. “Justice” may better communicate the public or judicial setting. A qualified explanation such as “conduct that is right before God” can help, provided it is not inserted as though it were a one-word equivalent. For mishpat, translate the actual judgment, ruling, ordinance, or due at issue.

The default explanation is that righteousness involves rightness in relation to the relevant standard and relationships. It is neither automatically moral perfection in every description of a person nor automatically a coded synonym for one doctrine of imputation. “Righteousness of God” also needs grammatical and argumentative analysis: God's quality, activity, faithfulness, and the righteousness given to believers are related proposals, not meanings established merely by an English possessive.

#### Biblical and canonical development

A focused trajectory links God's just character, Israel's responsibility to practice justice, Jesus' account of genuine righteousness, and Paul's proclamation of God's righteousness in Christ. Each stage contributes something specific. It should not be presented as if Moses, Matthew, and Paul were answering one identically worded question.

The working synthesis rejects a separation in which private piety has no public responsibilities. It also rejects using that synthesis to remove the judicial dimension of Paul's argument. Review the passage's agents, objects, contrast, and promised result. Righteousness, justice, justification, and mercy should be linked as study entries while their actual lexical records remain separate.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Follow God's character, Israel's social vocation, Jesus' teaching, and Paul's argument with local contexts intact.

### Full-depth semantic inventory

**Multiple lemmas, not one “righteousness” word.** Hebrew צֶדֶק (*tsedeq*) and צְדָקָה (*tsedaqah*) describe rightness, righteous conduct, just treatment or intervention according to syntax; מִשְׁפָּט (*mishpat*) can be verdict, judgment, entitlement, decision or legal practice. Greek δικαιοσύνη (*dikaiosynē*) and δίκαιος (*dikaios*) are related but distinct nouns/adjectives. “Justice” and “righteousness” both overlap; no rule allocates all public action to one Hebrew root and all private morality to another.

**Subjects and recipients.** Gen 18:19 describes Abraham's household practising righteousness and justice as part of a community calling. Deut 16:18–20 places those notions in judges' decisions and forbids partiality and bribes; the adversely affected parties matter. Isa 1:16–20 addresses wrongdoers and the vulnerable in the context of repentance. Matt 6:1 discusses the *practice* of righteousness before onlookers for recognition, not the same question as Rom 3:21–26 about God's saving righteousness in Christ. In Matthew 6:1, SBLGNT's δικαιοσύνη and an almsgiving-specific wording in other textual traditions create a genuine textual comparison, not merely an alternate dictionary gloss.

**Semantic neighbors and theology.** God's justice, God's fidelity, human moral conduct, judicial verdict and a gift of righteousness can be brought into conversation canonically; treating them as interchangeable erases the speakers and arguments. “Righteousness of God” is a grammatical expression whose possibilities require testing by the Romans discourse, rather than a single preselected theology of imputation or of relational harmony. **Coverage limit:** the listed lexemes span extensive legal, sapiential and prophetic usages; this study identifies supported families, not a complete occurrence inventory.
