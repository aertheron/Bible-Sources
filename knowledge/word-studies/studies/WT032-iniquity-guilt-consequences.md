# WT032 — Iniquity, guilt, consequences

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** ongerechtigheid, schuld, gevolgen.

**Original terms:** Hebrew עָוֹן (avon); Greek ἀνομία (anomia) and other possible renderings must be checked separately. Do not conflate related Hebrew verbs with this noun.

**Default explanation:** A crookedness illustration does not prove that every occurrence explicitly means distorted behavior. Related verb forms should not be counted as occurrences of the noun.

**Translation key:** Distinguish wrongdoing, guilt, and its burden or consequences when grammar and setting warrant that distinction. Retain ambiguity where a single English word cannot cover it.

**Initial orientation:** Exodus 34 coordinates iniquity, transgression, and sin. Leviticus 16 depicts bearing and removal of iniquities. Isaiah 53 uses iniquity language in its account of the servant and others.

#### Local reading

Avon can concern wrongdoing, guilt, or the consequence of wrongdoing. This range is especially important when the same English “iniquity” is used in clauses about committing, bearing, or having something removed. The verb and its object help identify whether the emphasis falls on the act, liability, or burden of punishment. Greek anomia contributes lawlessness or iniquity language but cannot be assumed to be the exact equivalent in every translation.

Genesis 4:13 places avon in Cain's response to the announced consequence of his violence. Renderings emphasizing his punishment being too great to bear and renderings emphasizing his guilt need to be assessed against the following statements about expulsion, vulnerability, and protection. This is a genuine contextual ambiguity; replacing the noun with an etymological image of crookedness does not resolve it.

Exodus 34:7 joins avon with other wrongdoing vocabulary and with forgiveness and accountability. Leviticus 16:20–22 describes confession and the bearing away of iniquities in a ritual setting. Isaiah 53:5–6 concerns the wrongdoing of others and its relation to the servant's suffering. Each passage contributes to a theology of sin and its removal through its predicates and images; the noun itself does not specify a complete mechanism of transferred guilt or punishment.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use iniquity, wrongdoing, guilt, or punishment where the construction supports the selected sense, marking consequential alternatives in a footnote. Dutch *ongerechtigheid*, *schuld*, and *straf* should remain distinct candidates. If traditional wording obscures the object being borne or removed, add a short explanation rather than pretending one Dutch or English noun covers every use without qualification.

The default explanation is that the word can connect wrongdoing with its resulting liability or burden. “Crookedness” may illustrate a historical association, but it is not the full meaning activated everywhere. A derived verb and the avon noun must remain separate corpus records; counting all related forms as the noun gives a false concordance.

#### Biblical and canonical development

Psalm 32:1–5 connects confession, forgiveness, and relief from guilt. Ezekiel 18:19–23 emphasizes personal responsibility in a debate about inherited consequences. These are important comparisons for any account that conflates responsibility for an act with experiencing its social or historical effects. Consequences can involve others without the text assigning them identical guilt.

The useful trajectory concerns wrongdoing, burden, confession, and divine removal. Isaiah's servant passage may provide a further explicit bridge where the user's study involves atonement, but it should not automatically control every avon occurrence. The working synthesis preserves both accountability and forgiveness. Theology review should ask what is borne, by whom, and with what result in the selected unit, distinguishing ritual imagery, poetic description, and direct moral claims. A translation should leave those relations available for examination rather than build a favored atonement theory into every occurrence.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Study responsibility, bearing guilt, removal, and restoration while distinguishing poetic images from a complete atonement theory.
