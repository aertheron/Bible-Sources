# WT008 — Freedom, willing, choice, responsibility

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** vrije wil, vrijheid, kiezen, verantwoordelijkheid.

**Original terms:** Greek ἐλευθερία (eleutheria), ἐλεύθερος (eleutheros), θέλημα (thelēma), θέλω (thelō); several Hebrew willing/choosing constructions. Free will is a question spanning texts, not a single lexical equivalent.

**Default explanation:** Moral responsibility, freedom from sin, and metaphysical accounts of alternative possibilities are distinct questions. A lexical definition alone does not prove determinism or libertarian freedom.

**Translation key:** Translate willingness, intention, freedom, and choosing in their actual settings. Do not replace all freedom language with a philosophical term.

**Initial orientation:** Deuteronomy 30 summons Israel to choose life. John 8 concerns slavery to sin and freedom given by the Son. Philippians 2 links the community's activity with God working in willing and acting. James 1 distinguishes temptation from God's goodness and traces desire's development.

#### Local reading

Biblical freedom language usually specifies a bondage, restriction, or relation from which someone is free. John 8:31–36 concerns slavery to sin and freedom given by the Son. It is not first a technical assertion about the philosophical ability to choose otherwise in every possible circumstance. Willing language identifies desire, intention, or what is willed; it does not by itself explain the causal origin of a decision.

Deuteronomy 30:15–20 presents a covenant summons to choose life. Its commands, consequences, and appeal establish responsibility in that setting. Philippians 2:12–13 addresses a community's activity while locating willing and acting within God's work. Reading the exhortation without the reason that follows, or the divine activity without the exhortation it supports, distorts the unit. Whether these passages support a particular philosophical model requires an argument beyond their vocabulary.

The testing example helps clarify agency. Genesis 22:1 uses nasah for God's testing of Abraham. Matthew 6:13 asks God not to bring the petitioners into peirasmos. James 1:13 denies that God tempts anyone, with verses 14–15 tracing desire, sin, and death. James 1:2–4 and 1:12 use the testing family in a discussion of trials and endurance. Matthew 4:1 distinguishes the Spirit's leading from the devil's tempting. The same Greek family can name an ordeal and its morally dangerous dimension; this is not a contradiction resolved by inventing unrelated Greek words.

#### Translation and default explanation

Preserve the Matthew petition's agency and negation. “Do not bring us into temptation” is defensible; “testing” or “trial” may clarify an aspect of the sense but needs a note because the moral danger remains important. Do not substitute “do not tempt us” as though it were the exact source clause. James' apeirastos warrants a separate note: “not susceptible to temptation by evil” is a contextual rendering, while its lexical history also includes being untried or inexperienced.

The default explanation distinguishes trial from inducement to evil and distinguishes circumstances from the agent who entices. This is a reasoned reconciliation, not proof that every difficulty disappears through one translation change. It also illustrates why grammar and context belong ahead of theology review.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The most useful trajectory is divine initiative and deliverance giving rise to responsible response. A second, only where relevant, follows testing, faithful endurance, and temptation resisted. Neither should trigger a canon-wide inquiry into every choice.

The working synthesis should affirm the summons and responsibility the passage states while explaining the grace or divine activity it names. Determinism and libertarian freedom are later analytical frameworks, not English equivalents of eleutheria. Evaluate a preferred account through a bounded set of commands, promises, narratives, and explicit causal claims. Avoid treating responsibility, freedom from sin, and alternative possibilities as identical questions.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Assess divine initiative and human response together, including commands, promises, warnings, and grace. Link to the testing/temptation reconciliation example below.
