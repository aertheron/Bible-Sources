# WT031 — Transgression, rebellion, breach

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** overtreding, rebellie, trouwbreuk.

**Original terms:** Hebrew פֶּשַׁע (pesha), פָּשַׁע (pasha); Greek παράβασις (parabasis), παράπτωμα (paraptōma). Verify actual correspondences passage by passage.

**Default explanation:** These words overlap with sin language but are not interchangeable in every legal, political, or relational setting.

**Translation key:** Use transgression, rebellion, offense, or breach as the local relationship and action warrant; preserve the distinct terms where an author coordinates them.

**Initial orientation:** Exodus 34 names wrongdoing that Yahweh forgives while addressing judgment. Isaiah 1:2 frames rebellion through a parental relationship. Ephesians 2 speaks of trespasses and sins within its description of former life.

#### Local reading

Transgression and rebellion vocabulary can highlight a breach of command, relationship, or authority. Pesha and pasha are Hebrew noun and verb records; parabasis and paraptōma are distinct Greek records. Their frequent overlap with sin language does not establish a rigid taxonomy in which every word always identifies one exclusive kind of offense. The specific command, relationship, and narrative response are important evidence.

Exodus 34:7 includes transgression alongside iniquity and sin in the description of forgiveness and the treatment of wrongdoing. The accumulation can communicate comprehensiveness without requiring that each noun represent an entirely separate legal category. Isaiah 1:2 places rebellion within parent-child and covenant language: the people's conduct is evaluated in relation to the God who raised them. The image supplies the relational force rather than merely illustrating a dictionary definition.

Second Kings 1:1 uses rebellion language for Moab's revolt after Ahab's death. This political setting demonstrates why a word study must identify the authority and relationship concerned. It cannot simply translate every occurrence as rebellion against God. Ephesians 2:1–3 uses trespass and sin vocabulary in an account of the community's former condition and conduct; its theological force comes from the passage's description of death, influence, desire, and wrath.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use rebel, transgress, trespass, breach, or wrongdoing according to the construction. Dutch *opstand*, *overtreding*, and *vergrijp* likewise need a named setting. Parabasis often foregrounds crossing an established boundary or violating a command. Paraptōma can name a wrongful act without implying that the offender merely slipped accidentally. Illustrations from movement are useful teaching aids, not complete definitions of culpability.

The default explanation is that transgression concerns a violation whose seriousness the passage establishes. Romans 4:15 links transgression with law in its argument; that does not assert that no wrongdoing exists in any setting without a stated command. Romans 5:15–20 contrasts Adam's trespass with the gift through Christ. The contrast requires Paul's clauses and Adam–Christ reasoning, not an etymological assertion about a false step.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The useful trajectory follows command and covenant responsibility, breach, judgment, and forgiveness. A second route may connect Adam's trespass and Christ's gift where the New Testament author makes that comparison. The runtime should not retrieve both merely because the English word transgression appears.

The working synthesis treats rebellion as accountable opposition or breach in the relevant relation, while acknowledging that other sin terms can describe the same event from another angle. Theology review should identify the violated relation, the nature of the act, and the stated consequence. It should not make every trespass maximally deliberate, or every sin merely inadvertent, by assigning moral intention from the word alone. Keep this entry connected with sin, iniquity, repentance, and forgiveness so the passage can be studied through its actual combinations rather than a preloaded chart that the text never supplies.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Follow breached obligations, divine forgiveness, and renewal without making one secondary illustration the universal definition.

### Full-depth semantic audit: rebellion and transgression

Hebrew **פֶּשַׁע (*pesha‘*)** and **פָּשַׁע (*pasha‘*)** can frame wrongdoing as revolt, breach or rebellion; Greek **παράβασις (*parabasis*)** often stresses crossing a known boundary, while **παράπτωμα (*paraptōma*)** may emphasize trespass, lapse or offence. They are related through moral discourse but do not constitute one mechanically equivalent original word or one rigid theological category.

**Contexts and agents.** Exod 34:6–7 places rebellion alongside iniquity and sin in God's self-revelation. The coordination lists distinct but overlapping aspects of human wrongdoing; it does not force a technical three-stage sin taxonomy. Isa 1:2 portrays Israel as rebellious children against their nurturing father, allowing the prophetic family image to supply a relational dimension. Eph 2:1–5 describes people previously dead in trespasses and sins and then God's mercy; the argument's larger issue is renewal by grace, not simply accidental stumbling over a line.

**Boundary and canonical inference.** A specific command crossed may suit “transgression”; disloyal revolt may suit “rebellion”; an individual lapse or offence may suit “trespass.” The translator must identify speaker, violated norm, affected relationship and consequences. The idea that any one word always specifies high-handed deliberate sin while another always means unintended error needs local evidence, not etymological certainty. Canonically the prophets' covenant-breach imagery and Paul's account of transformed life can be connected without hiding differences in context. **Limit:** separate ancient Greek rendering histories of *pesha‘* must be checked occurrence by occurrence.
