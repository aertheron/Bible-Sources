# WT028 — Hope, waiting, expectation

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** hoop, wachten, verwachting.

**Original terms:** Hebrew יָחַל (yachal/yakhal), קָוָה (qavah); Greek ἐλπίς (elpis), ἐλπίζω (elpizō).

**Default explanation:** A theological description of assured Christian hope is not the automatic definition of every expect/hope verb in ordinary discourse.

**Translation key:** Distinguish waiting, expecting, and hoping; name the basis and object rather than supplying a generic optimistic mood.

**Initial orientation:** Psalm 130 relates waiting to God's word and redemption. Isaiah 40 relates waiting to renewed strength. Romans 8 places hope within suffering, creation's groaning, and future bodily redemption.

#### Local reading

Hope and waiting vocabulary directs attention toward an anticipated object or event. Yachal and qavah often concern waiting or hoping, while elpis and elpizō concern expectation or hope in Greek. Their occurrence alone does not establish that the expectation is justified, religious, or certain. The source's object, grounds, and evaluation must supply those details.

Psalm 130:5–8 ties waiting to Yahweh's word, loyal love, and redemption, using the image of watchmen awaiting morning. Isaiah 40:31 describes renewed strength for those waiting for Yahweh within a passage addressing weariness and divine power. The two settings give hope its grounds rather than promising that waiting is automatically passive or that every desired outcome will occur.

The lexical database preserves a qavah record concerning collecting alongside the waiting record. That distinction is important: an illustration about twisting a cord or gathering strands must not be presented as the actual meaning of every waiting occurrence. Historical derivations can be discussed as proposals, but the local use of the waiting verb must remain primary.

Romans 8:18–25 concerns suffering, creation's expectation, adoption, bodily redemption, and patient waiting for what is not yet seen. First Peter 1:3–9 connects living hope with Jesus' resurrection and an inheritance while acknowledging grief in trials. These arguments give Christian hope a particular foundation and horizon. They do not make “confident certainty” the dictionary meaning of every ordinary expect or hope verb.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use hope, expect, wait, or await according to the construction. Where “hope” sounds like a mere wish, explain the stated ground: God's promise or Christ's resurrection, for example. Dutch *hoop* and *verwachting* may communicate different emphases, while *wachten op* preserves waiting. Do not insert certainty into the translation when the narrator describes a human expectation whose fulfillment is not established.

The default explanation is that biblical hope looks toward what is not yet possessed, with its strength grounded in the object and promise involved. Hope and doubt are not simply alternate dictionary definitions. Romans 8's unseen horizon and endurance matter precisely because fulfillment is awaited.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The useful trajectory connects trust in God's word amid distress with resurrection and future renewal. Romans 5:1–5 adds a relationship among suffering, endurance, tested character, and hope, grounded in God's love. That development should not be turned into a claim that every painful event automatically improves someone or that grief indicates failed faith.

The working synthesis presents hope as sustained expectation rooted in God's faithful saving action, where the passage establishes that ground. Theology review asks what has been promised, what remains future, and what response is encouraged. It should avoid projecting a reader's preferred outcome onto the biblical promise. Select the relevant anchor instead of surveying every eschatological expectation whenever the word hope occurs.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Trace promise and patient expectation while retaining the future horizon and present suffering.

### Full-depth semantic audit: hope and waiting

Hebrew **יָחַל (*yaḥal*)** and **קָוָה (*qavah*)** can express waiting, hoping or expectant endurance; Greek **ἐλπίς (*elpis*)** and **ἐλπίζω (*elpizō*)** identify hope or placing hope in something. These are different lexical families, not a single direct equivalent. “Hope” may be confidently anticipated, anxiously awaited, or misplaced depending on the **object**, not because its dictionary meaning automatically promises certainty.

**Textual controls.** Ps 130:5–8 describes a speaker waiting for Yahweh's word and a community invited to hope in His redeeming goodness. The watchman comparison supplies persistence and expectation from literary design. Isa 40:28–31 locates waiting upon Yahweh amid exhaustion and weakness; the specific promise concerns renewed strength. Rom 8:18–25 describes groaning creation and believers awaiting redemption of the body: the very distinction between what is seen and what is hoped for establishes an unseen future object. None requires that every use of hope entail uncertainty about God's faithfulness.

**Canonical limits.** Hope, patience, trust, promise and faith overlap but are distinct: hope points forward to an awaited good, trust concerns reliance on someone, and endurance characterizes the waiting person's conduct. Translate “wait” when temporal expectation drives the scene, “hope” where anticipated benefit or ground of confidence is foregrounded. A canonical theology of resurrection hope comes from Christ's resurrection and Paul's argument, not etymology of *elpis*. **Limit:** negative/misplaced-hope usages and the Hebrew roots' full distribution require further concordance-based verification.
