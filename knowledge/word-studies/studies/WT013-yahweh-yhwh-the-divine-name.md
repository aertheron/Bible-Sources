**Original terms:** Hebrew יהוה (YHWH, rendered Yahweh in this project), יהּ (Yah); related אֶהְיֶה (ehyeh) in Exodus 3. The name and verbal form must not be conflated.

#### Local reading

YHWH is the personal divine name in the Hebrew text, conventionally represented here as Yahweh. It should be distinguished from titles such as God, Lord, and Almighty. The shorter Yah is a related name form. A source record must identify the actual spelling, attached particles, and any reading convention rather than reconstructing the name from an English “LORD” after the fact.

Exodus 3:12–15 places the disclosure of the name in a scene of commission and promised presence. Ehyeh in verse 12 can naturally concern God's being with Moses; the related expression in verse 14 raises choices such as “I am” and “I will be.” The narrative associates the divine name with these verbal expressions, but the exact historical etymology of YHWH is debated. BDB's life-giver or causative proposal is a historical scholarly interpretation, not conclusive grammatical proof that the name must mean “He causes to be.”

Exodus 6:2–8 connects knowledge of the name with God's covenantal action and liberation. Explaining this passage requires considering the narrative's earlier uses of the name and what knowing it means in the scene. A word study should not resolve that literary question merely by repeating a proposed etymology. Identity, faithful presence, promise, and action together make the name significant.

#### Translation and default explanation

Render the Hebrew name as Yahweh under the project's stated convention, with a brief initial explanation. Preserve the distinction between YHWH and adon/adonai; combinations should remain visible. If another target language convention is requested, record the substitution so a reader can still identify the source term.

The default explanation is that Yahweh names the God of Israel rather than serving as a generic word for any deity. Greek kyrios is often used in the reception of divine-name texts, but it also serves for human masters and respectful address. A Greek occurrence cannot automatically be reverse-translated as Yahweh. The quotation's Hebrew source, the Greek wording, and the current speaker's referent must be compared.

#### Biblical and canonical development

Joel 2:32 and Romans 10:9–13 provide a focused divine-name trajectory. Paul's argument about confessing Jesus as Lord and calling on the Lord uses a scriptural promise concerning Yahweh. Isaiah 45:22–23 and Philippians 2:9–11 provide another significant reception. These are strong Christological connections because of the quotation and its use, not because every occurrence of “Lord” is a divine-name claim.

The working synthesis treats these applications as important evidence for the New Testament's presentation of Jesus within God's saving authority and identity. A theology review should trace the actual application, preserve the distinction between Father and Son in the passage, and explain the argument's force. It should not make an unverified name etymology carry a doctrine or label every use of kyrios identically.

### Full-depth semantic audit: name, presence and identity

The divine name **יהוה (YHWH, rendered Yahweh)** is a proper designation, not an interchangeable common noun for “God” or “Lord.” Hebrew **יָהּ (Yah)** is a shorter form in appropriate names or poetry; **אֶהְיֶה (*’ehyeh*, I will be / I am)** in Exod 3:12–15 is a verbal form in the name-disclosure narrative. The phonetic and theological connection between *’ehyeh* and YHWH is important but does not establish that the name is literally the full sentence “I am who I am” at every occurrence.

**Speaker and literary setting.** In Exod 3:12 Yahweh promises His presence with Moses; Exod 3:14–15 connects the name disclosure with deliverance and remembrance across generations. The Hebrew wording in v. 14 permits interpretive discussion of “I am” and “I will be,” while v. 12 gives the promise's temporal and relational force. The name is given in a concrete encounter, not as a stand-alone abstract ontology. In passages saying “call on the name of Yahweh,” the action is appeal and recognition; it does not invoke an etymology.

**Canonical reception.** Joel 2:32's invocation of Yahweh is reused in Rom 10:9–13 in a discourse about confessing Jesus as Lord and calling for salvation. This is a substantial scriptural and Christological connection, but translating every Greek *kyrios* back to the proper name would ignore its many human and divine contexts. **Uncertainty:** the name's earliest vocalization and disputed etymological reconstructions cannot be established from the consonants alone; “Yahweh” is the project's chosen scholarly convention, not a directly preserved ancient audio recording.
