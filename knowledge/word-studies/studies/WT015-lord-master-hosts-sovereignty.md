# WT015 — Lord, master, hosts, sovereignty

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** Heer, heer, meester, heerschappij.

**Original terms:** Hebrew אָדוֹן (adon), אֲדֹנָי (Adonai); Greek κύριος (kyrios). Linked titles: Yahweh Sabaoth / Yahweh of hosts; παντοκράτωρ (pantokratōr).

**Default explanation:** Kyrios does not always mean the divine name. Conversely, its use in a divine-name quotation should not be flattened to polite human address.

**Translation key:** Choose Lord, lord, master, or an explanatory title from the actual referent. Explain capitalization as an editorial choice rather than an original Greek distinction.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 24:18 contains a human address as lord. First Samuel 1:3 uses Yahweh of hosts. Psalm 110 distinguishes Yahweh and my lord. Philippians 2 places the confession of Jesus as Lord within exaltation and scriptural language.

#### Local reading

Lord and master vocabulary can name ownership, authority, respectful address, or divine sovereignty. In Genesis 24:18, the polite address to Abraham's servant does not identify him as Yahweh. Adon and related forms therefore need their actual referents. Adonai functions prominently in address to God, while a source record must still distinguish it from the divine name and from other inflected lord forms.

Psalm 110:1 is a particularly important case: Yahweh speaks to “my lord.” Rendering both simply as Lord can hide the two Hebrew expressions and the relation between speaker and addressee. Matthew 22:41–46 uses the psalm in a question about the Messiah's relationship to David. The force of the argument depends on more than the dictionary range of lord; it depends on whose lord is being described and on the scriptural scene.

Yahweh of hosts combines the divine name with army or host vocabulary. First Samuel 1:3 places this title in a worship setting. Context must determine the relevance of earthly armies, heavenly hosts, or comprehensive sovereignty; the word “hosts” does not automatically specify one particular angelic hierarchy. Pantokratōr contributes a Greek expression of sovereign power, notably in Revelation, but it should retain its own record rather than be treated as a mechanically identical translation of every Hebrew title.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use Lord, master, or sir where the actual relationship warrants it. Retain Yahweh for the Hebrew divine name under the project convention. In interlinear presentation, identify both words in Psalm 110:1 rather than producing two unexplained “Lord” rows. Explain “Yahweh of hosts” on first use without substituting an unverified full theological paraphrase into the text.

The default explanation is that kyrios can be an ordinary title or carry the significance of a divine-name quotation. The word alone does not decide which. A New Testament citation of a Yahweh passage applied to Jesus needs its source and reception identified; a polite vocative in dialogue should not receive that same explanation merely because its spelling matches.

#### Biblical and canonical development

Philippians 2:9–11 applies Isaiah 45:22–23's universal homage language to the confession of Jesus as Lord. Romans 10:9–13 similarly connects confession, invocation, and scriptural promise. These are useful bounded trajectories for divine-name Christology, and they provide stronger evidence than an unsorted concordance of kyrios.

The working synthesis explains Jesus' exalted authority in the text's relation to God's sovereignty while preserving its statements about the Father and the Son. Review whether a proposed translation has either flattened a significant quotation into ordinary social address or inflated a routine address into a divine-identity claim. The same caution applies to human lordship: its presence describes authority without necessarily approving every exercise of that authority.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Assess authority, kingship, and divine-name reception through context and identifiable scriptural connections.

### Full-depth semantic audit: lordship and masters

Hebrew **אָדוֹן (*’adon*)** can identify a human superior or master; **אֲדֹנָי (*’adonay*)** is a divine form of address in relevant contexts. Greek **κύριος (*kyrios*)** can address a human lord/master, function as a respectful title, or denote God or Jesus; actual referent decides, not the mere presence of the word. **Yahweh of hosts** describes divine lordship by a separate Hebrew name-plus-title construction. Greek **παντοκράτωρ (*pantokratōr*)**, commonly “almighty/all-sovereign,” is distinct from kyrios and from a generic human “master.”

**Actors and texts.** In Gen 24:18 the addressee called lord is a human participant; inferring divine status from the gloss would be absurd. 1 Sam 1:3 speaks of Yahweh of hosts in a worship setting, invoking God's rule over hosts without deciding from this one clause precisely which “hosts” are being pictured. Ps 110:1 differentiates Yahweh and the speaker's “my lord”; a contextual royal figure and later messianic reception are distinct analytic stages. Phil 2:9–11 confesses Jesus as **Lord** within exaltation and scriptural echoes: its theological force derives from Isaiah's worship language and the argument, not simply because *kyrios* is inherently divine.

**Rendering rules.** Keep proper name Yahweh distinct from the Hebrew title Adonai and Greek kyrios. Later manuscript practices or translation conventions that use “LORD” to represent YHWH must not be mistaken for evidence that the Hebrew original wrote *’adon*. **Limit:** broad *kyrios* social address and Septuagint substitution patterns require occurrence-specific control and cannot be decided through English capitalization.
