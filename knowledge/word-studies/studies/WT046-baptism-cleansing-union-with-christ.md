# WT046 — Baptism, cleansing, union with Christ

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** doop, reiniging, verbondenheid met Christus.

**Original terms:** Greek βαπτίζω (baptizō), βάπτισμα (baptisma), ἐπερώτημα (eperōtēma), and constructions with ἐν Χριστῷ (en Christō). Distinguish vocabulary, ritual description, and theological account.

**Default explanation:** The word alone does not establish every disputed baptismal practice or efficacy claim. Water baptism and the Spirit's gift are related without being lexically identical.

**Translation key:** Translate baptism while explaining its relevant imagery. Preserve appeal/pledge alternatives for eperōtēma until their contextual and lexical evidence is reviewed.

**Initial orientation:** Romans 6 relates baptism to Christ's death and new life. First Corinthians 12 concerns the Spirit and one body. First Peter 3 links baptism with conscience and Christ's resurrection. Acts 2 and 10 present different narrative settings for baptism and the Spirit's gift.

#### Local reading

Baptizō and baptisma belong to washing, immersion, and baptism language, including metaphorical applications. The word's physical associations are useful evidence, but an account of the Christian rite must include its participants, action, confession, and the relation the passage gives to Christ and the Spirit. A dictionary definition alone does not establish every disputed practice, recipient, or claim about efficacy.

Matthew 3:1–12 places John's baptism within repentance, confession, and preparation for the coming figure. Acts 2:38 connects repentance and baptism with forgiveness and the promised Spirit. Acts 10:44–48 describes the Spirit's coming upon hearers before Peter commands water baptism. These narrative sequences are important and should remain visible. They should not be reduced to a single mechanically repeated order by rearranging the account or treating water and Spirit as the same lexical referent.

Romans 6:1–11 relates baptism into Christ to his death, burial, resurrection, and a changed way of living. Its argument answers a question about continuing in sin; the ethical conclusion belongs to the baptismal account. First Corinthians 12:12–13 connects baptism in one Spirit with incorporation into one body across social distinctions. Galatians 3:26–29 connects being baptized into Christ with being clothed with Christ, belonging, and Abraham's offspring. The theological relations come from those assertions, not merely from the verb's origin.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use baptize or a natural target-language equivalent for the established rite, with a note where physical action or metaphorical force matters. Dutch *dopen* should preserve the distinction between water descriptions and Spirit expressions. Interlinear alignment must keep prepositions and their complements visible rather than substitute “symbol of” or “automatically causes” for constructions that do not contain those words.

First Peter 3:18–22 includes a comparison involving Noah and says that baptism now saves through Jesus' resurrection, while denying that the relevant action is merely removal of dirt from flesh. Eperōtēma can concern a request or appeal, and has been interpreted as a pledge or undertaking in this setting. The relation of a good conscience to God is part of the construction needing assessment. The brief STEP gloss “pledge” does not settle the question; its fuller lexical definition and the passage's wording must be considered together.

The default explanation is that New Testament baptism is connected with response to God, belonging to Christ, and renewed life, with different passages specifying different relations. The lexical record cannot alone decide a sacramental or symbolic theory. The Peter passage's strong saving formulation should neither be erased nor explained apart from its explicit reference to resurrection.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The useful trajectory links repentance and cleansing, participation in Christ's death and risen life, and the formation of a Spirit-enabled community. The project's covenant-and-new-creation interpretation is a possible synthesis of these links; it should be presented as an argument from the passages rather than as baptizō's definition.

The working study should show its preferred account and its strongest challenge, including Acts' differing sequences and Peter's wording. Theology review asks which claims concern God's action, the recipient's response, the rite, and its signification. It must not make water's presence or absence in one clause decide every other passage automatically. Keep a supported working translation with substantial notes where eperōtēma or a prepositional construction is decisive.

### Full-depth semantic audit: baptism and associated vocabulary

Greek **βαπτίζω (*baptizō*)** can designate dipping/immersing, washing or ritual baptizing depending on construction and institution; **βάπτισμα (*baptisma*)** is the associated rite. **ἐπερώτημα (*eperōtēma*)** in 1 Pet 3:21 is contested (appeal, request, undertaking/pledge), and **ἐν Χριστῷ** is a relational phrase, not a baptismal noun. Theological participation in Christ cannot be deduced from the basic physical verb alone.

**Participants and actions.** Rom 6:1–11 addresses people baptized into Christ's death and narrates the consequence as a new way of life. The “into Christ/into His death” syntax supplies union imagery not contained in the bare verb *baptizō*. Acts 2:38–41 portrays repentance and baptism in Pentecost's public summons; Acts 10:44–48 depicts reception of the Spirit before the accompanying water baptism. These narratives must not be forced into an absolute chronological rule from one passage. 1 Cor 12:13 connects Spirit and one body; its Greek prepositions and corporate subject need close reading before equating it with every description of water baptism. 1 Pet 3:18–22 relates baptism to conscience and resurrection while explicitly distinguishing the rite from mere removal of dirt.

**Competing covenant readings.** Baptism as covenantal formalization and new creation is a theological synthesis with real support from these passages, not a separate lexical sense of the washing verb. The lexical data alone neither settles paedobaptism versus believer-only baptism nor erases the commanded human response. Mark disputed *eperōtēma* honestly and read its phrase before deciding “pledge” versus “appeal.” **Limit:** Jewish ritual washings, early-Christian liturgical history and lexical nuance of all prepositions remain to be independently checked.
