# WT014 — God, divine designations, and titles

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** God, goden, goddelijke benamingen.

**Original terms:** Hebrew אֵל (El), אֱלֹהִים (elohim), אֱלוֹהַּ (eloah); Aramaic אֱלָהּ (elah); Greek θεός (theos). Separate title records: El Shaddai, El Elyon, Ancient of Days / Aramaic עַתִּיק יוֹמִין (attiq yomin).

**Default explanation:** A plural form alone does not prove the Trinity, and a shared designation does not give every referent equal identity or status.

**Translation key:** Identify the referent through grammar, narrative, and contrast. Preserve separate title explanations instead of reducing all titles to a single proposed etymology.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 1 identifies the Creator. Genesis 14 uses El Elyon in Melchizedek's blessing. Genesis 17 uses El Shaddai in a covenant setting. Daniel 7 presents the Ancient of Days in a vision. First Corinthians 8 distinguishes other claims to deity from its confession.

#### Local reading

El, elohim, eloah, Aramaic elah, and Greek theos are distinct lexical records used in divine designations. Their reference depends on the clause and setting. Elohim has a plural form but often takes singular syntax when referring to Israel's God. Its morphology alone is therefore not proof of a plurality of persons within God. Nor does the word's application to other figures automatically make those figures equal to Yahweh.

Genesis 1:1 names God as the agent of creation. Genesis 14:18–22 includes El Elyon, the Most High, and identifies Abram's allegiance in the narrative. Genesis 17:1 uses El Shaddai in a covenant scene. “God Almighty” is an established rendering of Shaddai, but proposed derivations involving sufficiency, mountains, or other historical associations should not be asserted as settled lexical facts. A translation can preserve the title and footnote uncertainty where its derivation matters.

Daniel 7:9–14 uses Aramaic, not Hebrew, for the Ancient of Days and the human-like figure before him. Attiq concerns being aged or advanced; the visionary title and its setting communicate authority and judgment. Psalm 82 and Exodus 7:1 complicate simplistic assertions that a divine designation has only one possible type of referent. They require local analysis rather than a list of English “God” occurrences treated as interchangeable evidence.

#### Translation and default explanation

Translate God, god, divine beings, or another warranted description according to the actual referent and syntax. Modern capitalization expresses interpretation; it is not independent ancient manuscript evidence. Keep El Shaddai, El Elyon, and Ancient of Days as title records linked to their component lemmas, without flattening the titles into generic synonyms.

The default explanation is that a designation's semantic range and a passage's assertion about its bearer are different questions. Calling someone elohim in a commissioned or judicial setting does not automatically identify that person as the Creator. Calling the Father God does not by itself settle every statement about the Son; the particular relations and predicates must also be read.

#### Biblical and canonical development

First Corinthians 8:4–6 places the confession of one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, in an argument about idols and allegiance. The parallel claims about all things being from the Father and through Jesus are central to its interpretation. Read those clauses together before importing a theological framework into either title.

The useful trajectory concerns the Creator's uniqueness, competing divine claims, and the New Testament's account of God and Christ. The working synthesis should show how exclusive worship and the passage's distinctions coexist. A theology review asks what identity, agency, relation, and worship each text actually attributes. Neither a plural ending nor a capitalization convention can substitute for that argument. Historical lexicon categories are useful lookup aids but should not be treated as a final resolution of contested divine-council or Christological passages.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Distinguish lexical designation, unique divine identity, and the canon's developed confession.
