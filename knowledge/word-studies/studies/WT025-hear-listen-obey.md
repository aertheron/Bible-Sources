# WT025 — Hear, listen, obey

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** horen, luisteren, gehoorzamen.

**Original terms:** Hebrew שָׁמַע (shama, shema as the command form); Greek ἀκούω (akouō), ὑπακούω (hypakouō).

**Default explanation:** The word family can include attentive response, but that does not make auditory hearing and obedience identical in every occurrence.

**Translation key:** Use hear, listen, heed, or obey where syntax and response establish the use. Explain the relation between reception and action without changing every hear verb into obey.

**Initial orientation:** Deuteronomy 6 begins with a summons to hear within instruction to love and teach. First Samuel 15:22 contrasts obedience and sacrifice. James 1 distinguishes hearing the word from doing it.

#### Local reading

Shama can concern hearing, attending, listening responsively, or obeying. Shema in Deuteronomy 6:4 is a command form, not a separate dictionary lemma whose meaning automatically differs from the verb family. The summons introduces a confession and whole-person love, followed by the repeated teaching and remembrance of God's words in ordinary life. Reading the command within verses 4–9 makes its responsive force clear.

First Samuel 15:22 contrasts obedience with sacrificial performance in the setting of Saul's failure. Here listening cannot be reduced to auditory perception: the contrast concerns responding to Yahweh's instruction. That contextual force does not mean every shama occurrence lexically entails obedience. Isaiah 6:9–10 speaks of hearing without understanding in a prophetic commission, showing why the senses and effects need to remain distinguishable.

Greek akouō likewise concerns hearing or listening in varied constructions. Hypakouō often concerns obedient response, but its range includes answering a knock at the door. A lexical database preserves these separate records and their settings. Mark 4:1–9's repeated hearing summons belongs to a parable whose narrative concerns varied reception; a theology of response comes from that developing argument rather than from a prefix alone.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use hear, listen, heed, or obey where the construction and contrast support it. “Hear, Israel” preserves the traditional opening of Deuteronomy 6; a note can explain that attentive response is expected. Translating it as “Obey, Israel” may express an aspect of the summons but obscures the speech-event metaphor and should not be treated as the only literal rendering.

The default explanation is that biblical listening often seeks understanding and response, yet hearing and obedience are not automatically identical senses. James 1:19–25 explicitly moves from readiness to hear toward doing the word and warns against hearing alone. That distinction should remain intelligible. Replacing every hear with obey would make the letter's contrast harder to explain and could falsely imply obedience has already occurred whenever someone hears.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The useful trajectory follows God's address, the people's reception, and embodied response. Romans 10:14–17 connects proclamation, hearing, and faith; it gives a concrete account of how the message is encountered. Deuteronomy's teaching pattern provides another route where family, memory, and daily practice are relevant.

The working synthesis treats attention to God's word as a summons to faithful response, not merely information acquisition. A theology review should ask what the passage says about understanding, willingness, and action, and should not assume the presence of the hearing verb answers all three questions. For an application, identify what is being heard and what response the unit actually asks for. Link heart, faith, and word entries only when they clarify those relations rather than as an automatic cluster loaded for every hearing occurrence.
