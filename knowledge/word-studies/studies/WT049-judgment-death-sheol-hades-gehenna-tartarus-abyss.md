# WT049 — Judgment, death, Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, Tartarus, abyss

Version 0.2.0; 2026-10-05. Theme hub. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** oordeel, dood, Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, Tartarus, afgrond.

**Original terms:** Hebrew שְׁאוֹל (Sheol), מִשְׁפָּט (mishpat); Greek ᾅδης (Hades), γέεννα (Gehenna), ταρταρόω (tartaroō), ἄβυσσος (abyssos), κρίσις (krisis), θάνατος (thanatos). Distinct subrecords also cover ἀθανασία (athanasia), αἰών (aiōn), αἰώνιος (aiōnios), ἀπόλλυμι (apollymi).

**Default explanation:** Do not collapse these into one English hell category. Their distinction does not by itself decide every question of final punishment, consciousness, duration, or immortality.

**Translation key:** Retain Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, Tartarus, and abyss in relevant judgment/underworld passages, explaining each use. Render the verb in 2 Peter as an action involving Tartarus; do not pretend a noun occurs there. Check natural-depth uses of abyssos separately.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 37:35 uses Sheol in mourning. Luke 16 uses Hades in a story. Second Peter 2:4 uses the Tartarus verb for sinning angels. Revelation 9 uses the abyss; Revelation 20 distinguishes Hades, judgment, and the lake of fire.

#### Local reading

This hub preserves judgment and death terms that English “hell” can conceal. Sheol concerns death, the grave, or the realm of the dead in particular Hebrew settings. Genesis 37:35 places it in Jacob's grief for Joseph; it does not identify Joseph as a damned person. Psalm 16:10 places Sheol within confidence in God. The local image and speaker's claim matter more than an automatic final-punishment gloss.

Hades participates in Greek dead or underworld language, often in scriptural reception of Sheol. Luke 16:19–31 presents Hades in a narrative of reversal, warning, and the sufficiency of Moses and the prophets. Its description should be taken seriously while recognizing the literary setting and distinguishing it from a complete account of final judgment. Revelation 20:11–15 distinguishes Hades, the dead it gives up, judgment, and the lake of fire. These must remain separate in translation.

Gehenna appears in judgment sayings such as Matthew 10:28, where destruction of body and psychē is at issue. Its relation to the Valley of Hinnom and judgment imagery should be explained using identified scriptural or historical evidence. Do not make an unsupported story about a continuously burning rubbish dump carry the saying's interpretation. Retain Gehenna and explain the warning rather than silently substitute an entire later hell system.

Second Peter 2:4 uses tartarōsas, a participle of tartaroō, concerning sinning angels held for judgment. The source does not use the noun Tartarus. “Confined them to Tartarus” can make the action and associated designation visible, with a footnote explaining the verbal form. It does not describe every dead human being. Abyssos has deep-water and underworld uses; Revelation 9:1–11 and Romans 10:6–8 require their own contexts. A single location chart that merges all these terms would misrepresent the texts.

#### Translation and default explanation

Retain Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, Tartarus within the verbal rendering, and abyss in the relevant underworld or judgment settings. Give one brief local explanation on first use, not the whole hub every time. Dutch forms such as Sjeool, Hades, Gehenna, Tartarus, and afgrond should preserve the same distinctions.

Aiōn and aiōnios require separate duration records. Age-language is relevant, but aiōnios cannot be hardcoded as always finite. It modifies life, punishment, redemption, judgment, and other subjects in different constructions. Matthew 25:46 uses it for both punishment and life. That parallel deserves substantial weight. Whether punishment's enduring character describes an ongoing experience or an irreversible result requires the subject and wider argument; choosing “age-long” does not by itself prove either account.

Apollymi can concern destroying, perishing, or losing; olethros can concern destruction or ruin. The object, voice, and result must be assessed before deriving a complete account of extinction. Kolasis concerns punishment in Matthew 25:46; a proposed pruning etymology or a classical distinction from another penalty term does not establish that this occurrence must be temporary rehabilitation. Conversely, “punishment” alone does not encode every detail of endless conscious torment.

The default explanation is that judgment is real and morally consequential, while the source uses multiple images and terms whose relationships need interpretation. Their difference corrects translation flattening; it does not automatically settle duration, consciousness, finality, or the intermediate state.

#### Biblical and canonical development

First Timothy 6:16 attributes immortality uniquely to God, while First Corinthians 15:53–54 describes its being received in resurrection. Together they support distinguishing God's intrinsic life from immortality given to human beings. They do not independently decide every question of continued personal existence after death.

The project's preferred working synthesis emphasizes life as God's gift, proportionate judgment, and final exclusion from life understood as the second death. Retain that account as a theological argument, rather than as the lexical meaning of Sheol, aiōnios, or destroy. Matthew 10:28, John 3:16, and Revelation 20 provide important support to evaluate. Its strongest challenges include Matthew 25:46, Revelation 14:9–11, Revelation 20:10, and Second Thessalonians 1:9. Explain their language, subjects, genre, and relation to the proposed conclusion rather than dismissing them through a gloss.

Hebrews 6:2 and Jude 1:7 supply useful comparisons concerning eternal judgment and fire. Comparison of an enduring result with an ongoing action is a reasoned interpretive proposal, not a lexical rule proved by the adjective alone. A theology review should state the preferred synthesis, its evidence, and any unresolved challenge plainly. For runtime retrieval, select the actual term and no more than the relevant two comparison routes; this large hub is not an instruction to analyze all afterlife passages whenever a death word occurs.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Record Mark's relational de-creation account as the project's proposed synthesis with its support and counter-evidence. Preserve genre, temporal setting, and alternative readings for contested texts.
