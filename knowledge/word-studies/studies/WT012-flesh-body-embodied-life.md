# WT012 — Flesh, body, embodied life

Version 0.3.0; 2026-10-09. Word family. English core with Dutch search aliases.

**Dutch aliases:** vlees, lichaam, lichamelijk leven.

**Original terms:** Hebrew בָּשָׂר (basar); Greek σάρξ (sarx), σῶμα (sōma). Different original words can be hidden by body/flesh paraphrases.

**Default explanation:** The flesh in John 1:14 is not a claim that Jesus assumed moral evil. Paul's negative use does not make embodiment itself evil.

**Translation key:** Use flesh or body when warranted, and explain Paul's contextual flesh-language rather than inserting sinful nature automatically.

**Initial orientation:** Genesis 2:24 concerns one flesh. Isaiah 40 contrasts vulnerable human life with God's enduring word. John 1:14 concerns the Word becoming flesh. Romans 8 uses flesh within an ethical opposition to the Spirit. First Corinthians 6 concerns the body's relation to Christ.

#### Local reading

Basar, sarx, and sōma contribute different but overlapping accounts of embodied life. Basar may concern bodily flesh, kinship, or humanity's frailty. Genesis 2:24's one-flesh expression belongs to the account of man and woman, leaving and joining, and the establishment of a new bond. Isaiah 40:6–8 compares flesh with grass to emphasize mortality in contrast with God's enduring word. Neither passage supplies a universal definition of flesh as a sinful substance.

John 1:14 says the Word became flesh. In this setting, sarx emphasizes actual human existence. It cannot plausibly mean that the Word became morally evil merely because Paul sometimes uses flesh negatively. Romans 8:1–13 contrasts life according to flesh with life according to the Spirit. The governing phrases, their objects, and the argument about sin and life establish the ethical contrast; sarx does not have to mean an inherently evil physical body.

First Corinthians 6:12–20 gives the body a positive theological significance: it belongs to the Lord, is destined for resurrection, and must not be treated as morally disposable. First Corinthians 15:42–49 distinguishes a psychikos body and a pneumatikos body within resurrection reasoning. “Spiritual body” should not automatically be explained as an immaterial non-body. The adjective's relation to Spirit and the passage's contrasts need to be read within Paul's description of transformed bodily life.

#### Translation and default explanation

Use flesh where it preserves a meaningful source distinction, explaining the setting rather than always substituting “sinful nature.” That substitution can be an interpretive paraphrase in some Pauline passages, but it hides sarx and may imply that Paul condemns embodiment itself. Use body for sōma unless a particular construction warrants another expression; metaphorical body language requires identifying the collective referent.

The default explanation is that bodily existence is not synonymous with moral corruption. Flesh can highlight material life, kinship, weakness, or life opposed to the Spirit, with the local clause selecting the use. The New Testament's incarnation and resurrection affirm bodily life while confronting sin's power within it. Dutch *vlees* and *lichaam* should not be interchanged merely to make a sentence sound more spiritual.

#### Biblical and canonical development

The useful trajectory connects created embodied persons, human mortality, Christ's becoming flesh, and bodily renewal. A second trajectory may follow reliance on flesh versus life empowered by the Spirit. The two can interact without being identical sense histories.

The working synthesis rejects treating the body as a temporary, ethically insignificant container. It also rejects making “whole person” the dictionary meaning of every basar or sōma occurrence. Galatians 3:2–3 raises a flesh/Spirit contrast involving the community's attempt to complete what began through the Spirit; a bodily-material gloss alone would miss the argumentative setting. Theology review should check how the proposed anthropology explains incarnation, moral responsibility, death, and resurrection without forcing a single gloss into all four.

**Study questions for the theology check:** Connect creation, incarnation, ethical life, and resurrection, preserving the difference between lexical uses and anthropology.

### Full-depth semantic audit: flesh versus body

Hebrew בָּשָׂר (*basar*) can denote flesh, kinship/one flesh (Gen 2:24), vulnerable embodied humanity (Isa 40:6) or humanity broadly. Greek **σάρξ** (*sarx*) can denote physical flesh, human creaturely condition, family descent or an ethically opposed orientation (Rom 8), while **σῶμα** (*sōma*) refers to a body, sometimes collective or metaphorical according to context. A *sarx* verse does not by itself teach contempt for created embodiment.

**Different agents and genres.** In Gen 2:24 the man and woman become “one flesh” through a relational union; it is not a claim that literal tissue merges into one organism. Isa 40 contrasts all flesh as vulnerable with God's enduring word. John 1:14 says the Word **became flesh**, affirming actual human existence rather than automatically identifying Jesus with the moral “flesh” contrasted with Spirit in Paul's specific ethical argument. Rom 8:1–13 contrasts two orientations or spheres of life; 1 Cor 6:12–20 argues that believers' bodies belong to Christ and that sexual conduct matters. Physical bodies are not therefore evil.

**Theological distinctions.** Embodiment, fallenness, family kinship, bodily weakness, ethics and new creation are interrelated but distinguishable. The human *basar* and Greek *sarx* cannot be replaced wholesale by “sinful nature,” nor *sōma* by “flesh” without examining construction. Canonical Christology affirms incarnation and resurrection; ethical exhortation concerns behaviour and allegiance, not escaping material being. **Limit:** the full Paulinist idiom catalogue and all collective-body metaphors exceed the selected local text sample.
