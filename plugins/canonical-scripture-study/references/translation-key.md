# Translation policy and key 0.1.3

Label substantial output **Working translation**, or **Werkvertaling** in Dutch. Treat it as a study aid with revisable decisions, not an official translation. Produce natural requested-language prose while preserving defensible repetition, imagery, contrast, wordplay and argument. Keep source text, selected reading and contextual sense separate.

| Format | Display |
| --- | --- |
| plain_working | Readable working translation with necessary context and notes |
| transliteration_interlinear | Transliteration followed by contextual gloss; original script stays in the structured alignment data but need not be displayed |
| original_interlinear | Original form first, transliteration second, contextual gloss last |

Use actual inflected source forms and stable source word/token IDs. Align phrases where a one-word gloss is misleading. Preserve source order in the interlinear; follow natural target-language order in the working translation. Add a readable clause translation when the gloss line obscures syntax. Greek surface IDs are derived segmentation identifiers, not morphological analyses. Retrieve or carefully review morphology before asserting it; mark an inference as such.

Render Hebrew YHWH as **Yahweh**. Render Greek kyrios contextually as Lord/Master or another defensible sense; do not automatically replace every occurrence with Yahweh. Explain a warranted connection to an OT Yahweh text.

Preserve **Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, Tartarus and abyss** as distinct terms with concise first-use explanations. Do not collapse them into one “hell” concept. Distinguish the verb referring to Tartarus in 2 Peter 2:4 from a noun occurrence. Determine whether abyssos denotes a natural depth or a cosmic/prison context before supplying its explanation. Do not smuggle later cosmology into a gloss.

Translate nephesh/psychē contextually: life, person, living being, self and other senses depend on the occurrence. Do not force transliteration or “soul” everywhere. Keep the source term visible in the term key where a traditional target word could confuse the reading. Apply the same discipline to heart, spirit, flesh, faith, works, righteousness, justification and other common labels.

Check translation against the explanation: an argued sense of kabash as subdue/control must not silently become merely cultivate in Genesis 1:28. Render nephesh chayyah naturally in its sentence rather than the awkward gloss “living life.” A shared term for animals and humans supports a contextual living-being sense; it does not by itself settle every question about human ontology. Preserve defensible poetic choices such as “wild and waste” for tohu va-bohu while checking each occurrence.

Explain the grammar behind a consequential choice such as “When God began to create” in Genesis 1:1 and footnote the meaningful alternative. Do not choose syntax merely because it prevents an unwanted doctrinal inference, and do not claim either rendering by itself proves creation from nothing or eternal matter.

Do not derive a sentence's meaning by adding all senses of a root, by etymology alone or by later doctrinal usage. aiōnios does not mean either “endless” or “age-long” automatically in every occurrence. Explain a consequential ambiguity rather than hiding it beneath a preferred fixed gloss.

For each consequential key term, record the occurrence, actual form, lemma where sourced/reviewed, grammatical role, contextual sense, proposed target rendering, meaningful alternative and evidence. The existing 50 studies and contextual sense records are candidates; a complete reviewed occurrence-level key is not yet compiled. Reuse a reviewed entry only when its contextual conditions match.

Separate note categories: textual alternative, rendering/grammar, literary connection and historical context. State witness differences factually; avoid presenting an inferred motive as a fact. If an explanation concerns yod/vav confusion or Augustine's explanation of John 8, verify its relevance and attribution before attaching it. Footnote consequential rejected readings and mark bracketed material where it actually occurs. Do not add familiar translations of unattested material from memory.

Before translating a dependent opening, explain the prior argument or scene briefly from retrieved context. In Romans 12:1–2, retrieve Romans 11:32–36 as a starting window and review the wider preceding argument when needed. A context window is a retrieval aid, not proof that its five verses exhaust the rhetorical background.
